# Hallucination

Hallucination is when a model gives a response that isn't grounded in facts (AIE p.106). It predates the term '[[foundation-model]]' and the [[transformer-architecture]], having been noted in text generation as early as 2016 (Goyal et al., 2016), with detection and measurement a staple of natural language generation research since (Lee et al., 2018; Nie et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2020) (AIE p.107). Hallucinations are fatal for fact-dependent tasks; in June 2023 a law firm was fined for submitting fictitious legal research generated by ChatGPT (AIE p.107).

Sampling randomness alone doesn't explain hallucination, since a model can output something believed never to have appeared in its training data, and it's impossible to verify training data exhaustively (AIE p.107). Two hypotheses explain why it happens.

The first, from Ortega et al. (DeepMind, 2021), is that a model can't differentiate between given data and data it has itself generated -- a form of 'self-delusion.' Once a generated sequence drifts slightly off, the model conditions on its own prior output as if it were fact and can expand it into outrageously wrong claims (AIE p.108). Zhang et al. (2023) call the resulting chain 'snowballing hallucinations': an incorrect assumption compounds, and can even cause the model to answer questions wrong that it could otherwise answer correctly (AIE p.108-109). DeepMind's paper suggests two mitigations: a reinforcement-learning technique that trains the model to differentiate user-provided prompts ('observations') from its own generated tokens ('actions'), and a supervised-learning technique that includes factual and counterfactual signals in training data (AIE p.109).

The second hypothesis, from OpenAI's Leo Gao, is that hallucination stems from a mismatch between the model's [[internal-knowledge]] and the labeler's internal knowledge: during [[supervised-finetuning]], models mimic labeler-written responses that may draw on knowledge the model itself lacks, effectively teaching it to hallucinate (AIE p.109). OpenAI co-founder John Schulman expressed a similar view in an April 2023 UC Berkeley talk, and further argued that LLMs know if they know something; he proposed two fixes: verification, where the model retrieves the sources behind a response, and reinforcement learning with a reward function that punishes fabrication more directly, since the standard [[reward-model]] is trained only on pairwise comparisons without an explanation of why one response is better (AIE p.109-110). Schulman noted OpenAI found [[rlhf]] helps reduce hallucination, but the [[instructgpt]] paper shows RLHF actually made hallucination worse compared to supervised finetuning alone, even though human labelers still preferred the RLHF model overall (AIE p.110).

The two hypotheses complement each other: self-delusion centers on how [[self-supervision]] causes hallucination, while mismatched internal knowledge centers on how [[supervision]] causes it (AIE p.110). Prompt-based mitigations include instructing the model to answer truthfully and admit uncertainty, and asking for concise responses, since fewer generated tokens give it less room to fabricate; prompting and context-construction techniques discussed elsewhere in the book can also help (AIE p.110).

## Key figures
None. RLHF's effect on hallucination in InstructGPT is reported as a directional finding (worse, not better) rather than a specific figure (AIE p.110).

## Examples
- LLaVA-v1.5-7B misreads a shampoo bottle's label, convinces itself the product is milk, then lists milk as an ingredient extracted from the label (AIE p.108)
- A model claims 9677 is divisible by 13 after an initial wrong assumption, despite otherwise knowing this is false (AIE p.109)
