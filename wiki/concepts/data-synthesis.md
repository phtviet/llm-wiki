# Data Synthesis

Data synthesis is the use of AI models to generate training data. The book gives five
main reasons teams turn to it: increasing [[data-quantity]], increasing data coverage,
increasing data quality, mitigating privacy concerns, and distilling models (AIE p.381).

To increase **quantity**, synthesis offers an abundant supply of training and test data
where real-world data is scarce or hard to obtain, such as data for rare weather
conditions, deep sea exploration, or self-driving-car accidents (AIE p.381).

To increase **coverage**, synthesis can target specific characteristics: generating very
short or very long texts, toxic phrases for a toxicity detector (or safe data if
real-world data is toxic), adversarial examples, or extra examples for underrepresented
classes to address class imbalance. Anthropic's model-written evaluations work tested
154 distinct AI behaviors this way, and found LM-generated datasets approach or
sometimes exceed the quality of human-written ones (AIE p.381).

To increase **quality**, synthetic data can beat human-generated data where humans have
fundamental limitations: AI tool-use data can differ usefully from human tool-use
patterns, AI can generate math problems far more complex than an average human expert
would conceive, and AI-generated preference ratings can be more consistent than human
preference labels, which vary with mood and motivation across annotators (AIE p.382).

Synthetic data also **mitigates privacy concerns**, since it is often the only option
where real records can't be used at all -- synthetic patient records in healthcare or
synthetic claims in insurance avoid exposing sensitive personal and financial
information (AIE p.382).

Finally, synthesis is the mechanism behind **model distillation**: training a cheaper
or faster distilled model on data generated by the original (teacher) model (AIE p.382).
