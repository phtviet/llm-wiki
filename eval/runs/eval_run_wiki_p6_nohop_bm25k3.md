## Q1 What is quantization?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, which cuts its memory footprint. For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is cheap, effective, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures; "low precision" generally refers to any format with fewer bits than standard FP32 (AIE p.328).

Strictly speaking, quantization means converting to an integer format, but the term is commonly used — including in this context — to refer to any precision-reduction technique more broadly (AIE p.328).

**What gets quantized and when:**

Two decisions define a quantization approach: what to quantize, and when.

- **What**: Weights and activations are the major memory contributors during inference. Weight quantization is more common than activation quantization since it has a more stable impact on performance with less accuracy loss (AIE p.328).
- **When**: Quantization can happen post-training or during training. Post-training quantization (PTQ) — quantizing a model after it is fully trained — is by far the most common approach and the one most relevant to application developers who do not train models themselves (AIE p.328).

**Benefits and tradeoffs:**

Reduced precision also speeds up computation, allowing larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, which lowers both inference latency and training time — though format-conversion overhead can offset this gain (AIE p.329-330). The downside is accuracy loss: each precision conversion causes a small value change, many of which can compound into a large performance change, and out-of-range values can convert to infinity or an arbitrary value (AIE p.330).

Inference in lower precision is now standard practice: a model is trained in a higher-precision format to maximize performance, then quantized for inference, with PTQ offered for free by major frameworks such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Hugging Face transformers, as well as on-device frameworks like TensorFlow Lite and PyTorch Mobile (AIE p.330).

Training-time quantization is less common than PTQ but gaining traction, pursued for two distinct goals: producing a model that performs well in low precision at inference, or reducing training time and cost by training on cheaper hardware or training a larger model on the same hardware (AIE p.331). Quantization-aware training addresses the first goal, while training directly in lower precision (often via mixed-precision training) can address both goals but is harder since backpropagation is more sensitive to lower precision (AIE p.331).

Quantization has a floor — values cannot use fewer than 1 bit — though 1-bit approaches have been attempted, such as BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, and BitNet (AIE p.329).

## Q3 What is model distillation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term. The goal has traditionally been to produce smaller models for deployment: deploying a big model can be resource-intensive, and distillation can yield a smaller, faster student that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395).

The student model can be trained from scratch, like DistilBERT, or finetuned from a pre-trained model, like Alpaca (AIE p.395). Synthetic instruction data is commonly used together with adapter-based techniques such as LoRA; for example, BuzzFeed finetuned a Flan-T5 model using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, reducing inference cost by 80%, though how well the model performed was unclear (AIE p.395).

A key defining boundary: distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard. Training a student on a teacher's synthetic outputs to become larger or more capable than the teacher (for example NVIDIA's Nemotron-4) is therefore not distillation — this is what separates distillation from synthetic-data training in general (AIE p.396). There is also a practical limit on its use: many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models, so not all models can be distilled (AIE p.395).

Examples given:
- **DistilBERT**: a student trained from scratch, reducing BERT's size by 40%, retaining 97% of its language comprehension, and running 60% faster (AIE p.395).
- **Alpaca**: a student finetuned from a pre-trained model, built by finetuning Llama-7B on outputs from the 175-billion-parameter text-davinci-003, resulting in a model 4% the size of its teacher (AIE p.395).

## Q4 What's the difference between quantization and distillation?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Quantization and distillation are both model compression techniques, but they operate in fundamentally different ways:

**Quantization** reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint without changing the model's architecture or parameter count. For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is applied to weights and/or activations, and can happen either post-training (the most common approach) or during training (AIE p.328). Reduced precision also speeds up computation, enabling larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, though it carries a risk of accuracy loss from value changes during conversion (AIE p.329-330).

**Model distillation** (knowledge distillation) is a method in which an entirely separate, smaller model (the student) is trained to mimic a larger model (the teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015), as described on the model-distillation page. The knowledge of the big model is "distilled" into the small model. The student can be trained from scratch, like DistilBERT, or finetuned from a pre-trained model, like Alpaca (AIE p.395). The goal is to produce a smaller, faster model that retains performance comparable to the teacher, with the teacher's performance serving as the student's gold standard (AIE p.395-396).

**Key differences:**
- *Mechanism*: Quantization lowers the numerical precision of an existing model's values; distillation trains a wholly new (typically smaller) model from a teacher model's behavior/outputs (AIE p.328; model-distillation page).
- *Architecture*: Quantization does not change a model's architecture or parameter count, only how those parameters are numerically represented; distillation produces a genuinely different, smaller model, as noted explicitly in the pruning page's contrast ("quantization: reduces numerical precision rather than zeroing/removing parameters") and the model-distillation page's contrast ("lowers numerical precision vs. trains a small student").
- *Data requirements*: Distillation requires training data, often synthetic data generated by the teacher model (e.g., BuzzFeed finetuned Flan-T5 using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003) (AIE p.395); quantization does not require training a new model on teacher-generated outputs.
- *Licensing constraints*: Many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models, which limits which models can be distilled (AIE p.395) — this is not a constraint relevant to quantization.

Both are described as "main model-compression methods" (per the model-distillation page's "Related" section), but quantization works by compressing representation precision, while distillation works by compressing capability into a separate, smaller model trained on the original's outputs.

## Q7 What is RAG?

**Category:** factual

**A:** RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources—an internal database, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet (AIE p.253). Because instructions are common to all queries while context is specific to each query, RAG constructs context per query instead of reusing the same context for every query; the book likens this to feature engineering for classical ML models, since both serve to give a model the information it needs to process an input (AIE p.255).

**Origins:**
- The retrieve-then-generate pattern was first introduced in "Reading Wikipedia to Answer Open-Domain Questions" (Chen et al., 2017), where a system retrieves the five Wikipedia pages most relevant to a question and a reader model uses them to generate an answer.
- The term "retrieval-augmented generation" was coined in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020), proposed for knowledge-intensive tasks where all available knowledge cannot be input into the model directly; only the information the retriever judges most relevant is retrieved and input into the model. Lewis et al. found that access to relevant information helps a model generate more detailed responses while reducing hallucinations (AIE p.254).

**Architecture:** A RAG system has two components: a retriever that retrieves information from external memory sources, and a generator that generates a response based on the retrieved information (AIE p.256). In the original RAG paper, the retriever and generative model were trained together, but in today's systems the two components are often trained separately, with many teams building RAG systems from off-the-shelf retrievers and models (AIE p.256).

**Why RAG persists despite long-context models:** RAG emerged early as a way to overcome models' context-length limitations. The book argues a sufficiently long context will not end RAG's usefulness, for two reasons: available data only grows over time, so context length will never expand fast enough for every application's data needs; and a model that can process long context does not necessarily use that context well, since longer contexts make a model more likely to focus on the wrong part, and every extra context token adds cost and potential latency. RAG lets a model use only the most relevant information per query, cutting input tokens while potentially increasing performance (AIE p.255).

## Q8 What are embeddings?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Embeddings are numerical representations, in vector form, that aim to capture the meaning of the original data (AIE p.134). Since computers work with numbers, models convert input into embeddings before processing it; many models, including GPTs and Llamas, include a step to generate embeddings, typically within a [[transformer-architecture]]'s embedding layer (AIE p.134).

Key points about embeddings:

- **Quality criterion**: An embedding algorithm is considered good if more-similar inputs have closer embeddings, measured by cosine similarity or related metrics — for example, the embedding of "the cat sits on a mat" should be closer to "the dog plays on the grass" than to "AI research is super fun" (AIE p.135).

- **Utility-based evaluation**: Embedding quality can also be evaluated by its usefulness for downstream tasks; embeddings are used in classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and RAG (AIE p.135).

- **Not limited to text**: Ecommerce solutions have product embeddings, and Pinterest has embeddings for images, graphs, queries, and users (AIE p.135).

- **Multimodal embeddings**: A joint embedding space that represents data of different modalities is called a multimodal embedding space; in a text-image joint space, an image of a man fishing should embed closer to "a fisherman" than to "fashion show," enabling applications like text-based image search (AIE p.136).

- **Typical size**: Embedding vectors are typically between 100 and 10,000 elements (AIE p.134).

Examples of models that produce embeddings include BERT (base: 768, large: 1024 embedding size), CLIP (image and text embeddings, both size 512), and Sentence Transformers, an open source model trained specifically to produce embeddings (AIE p.134).

## Q9 What are the main techniques for inference optimization?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, inference optimization techniques can be organized at different levels and target different bottlenecks:

## Levels of Optimization

[[inference-optimization-levels]] describes three levels: **model**, **hardware**, and **service**, using an archery analogy—model-level is like crafting better arrows, hardware-level is like training a stronger archer, and service-level is like refining the whole shooting process (AIE p.426). Hardware-level techniques are outside the book's scope, so only model-level and service-level are discussed, and in production these are typically combined (AIE p.426).

## Model-Level Techniques

General model-level compression techniques mentioned include [[quantization]] and [[model-distillation]] (AIE p.426; p.43).

### Techniques addressing the autoregressive decoding bottleneck
Because autoregressive models generate tokens sequentially—and this is both slow and memory bandwidth-bound (AIE p.427-428)—several techniques aim to break or work around this dependency:

- **[[speculative-decoding]]**: A faster "draft" model generates K candidate tokens, which a more powerful "target" model verifies in parallel, accepting the longest agreeing subsequence (AIE p.428-429). This turns decoding's computation profile into something like prefilling's, and has been adopted in vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, and llama.cpp (AIE p.429).
- **Inference with reference**: listed as a technique addressing the same bottleneck, using input tokens as drafts (AIE p.428).
- **[[parallel-decoding]]**: Attempts to generate multiple future tokens simultaneously rather than one at a time, either via a single decoder repeatedly refining guesses (Lookahead/Jacobi decoding) or via separate decoding heads predicting specific future positions (Medusa), with generated tokens then verified and integrated (AIE p.431-432).

## Service-Level Techniques

- **[[batching]]**: Groups multiple simultaneous requests for joint processing to improve throughput, trading off some latency. Three variants exist: **static batching** (waits for a fixed batch to fill), **dynamic batching** (processes when the batch fills or a time window elapses, whichever first), and **continuous/in-flight batching** (returns completed responses immediately and slots in new requests, maximizing occupancy; introduced by the Orca paper) (AIE p.440-441).
- **[[prefill-and-decode]]** decoupling: addresses resource contention within request processing, since prefill is compute-bound while decode is memory bandwidth-bound (AIE p.409, referenced via [[computational-bottlenecks]]).

## Underlying Framework: Bottleneck Identification

Per [[computational-bottlenecks]], optimization fundamentally means identifying whether a workload is **compute-bound** or **memory bandwidth-bound** (via arithmetic intensity, per the Roofline paper) and applying matching techniques—more/faster chips for compute-bound workloads, higher-bandwidth hardware for memory bandwidth-bound ones (AIE p.407-408). Transformer inference splits into prefill (compute-bound) and decode (memory bandwidth-bound) steps, and most current AI workloads are memory bandwidth-bound given current accelerators and the prevalence of the transformer architecture (AIE p.409-410).

## Q11 What is backward propagation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Backpropagation is the mechanism most neural networks are trained with (AIE p.320). Each training step has two phases: a forward pass, which computes the output from the input, and a backward pass, which updates the model's weights using signals aggregated from the forward pass. During inference, only the forward pass runs; during training, both run (AIE p.320).

The backward pass works in three steps (AIE p.321):

1. **Compute the loss** — the computed output is compared against the expected output (ground truth); the difference is the loss.
2. **Compute the gradient** — how much each trainable parameter contributes to the loss is computed as the derivative of the loss with respect to that parameter, giving one gradient value per trainable parameter. A parameter with a high gradient contributes significantly to the loss and should be adjusted more.
3. **Adjust parameter values** — parameters are updated according to their gradients, with the adjustment size determined by an optimizer.

During the backward pass, each trainable parameter carries additional stored values — its gradient and its optimizer states — so more trainable parameters require more memory to train (AIE p.321).

## Q13 What are the best practices of prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki pages, prompt engineering best practices break into several categories:

## Writing Clear and Explicit Instructions

**1. Explain without ambiguity what the model should do.** For example, when grading an essay, specify the exact scoring scale (1-5 vs 1-10), whether the model should guess or say "I don't know" when uncertain, and whether fractional outputs like 4.5 are allowed. Prompts are typically refined iteratively — observing an undesirable output motivates an explicit instruction ruling it out (AIE p.220).

**2. Ask the model to adopt a persona.** This shifts the perspective used to generate a response. Example: an essay about liking chickens scores 2/5 from a model with no persona, but 4/5 when told to respond as a first-grade teacher (AIE p.220).

**3. Provide examples** (a form of [[few-shot-learning]]) to reduce ambiguity about the desired response. Example: a children's chatbot debunks Santa's existence when asked without examples, but affirms Santa once given a prior in-context example about the tooth fairy answered in-character (AIE p.221). Example formatting also has a cost dimension — a terser format ("chickpea --> edible") used 27 tokens versus 38 for a verbose input/output-labeled format for the same task on GPT-4, so the cheaper format should be preferred when performance is equal (AIE p.222).

**4. Specify the output format.** This matters for cost/latency (longer outputs cost more and increase latency) and for downstream parseability — e.g., telling the model to skip preambles, specifying JSON keys, and using explicit end-of-prompt markers so the model knows where to begin its structured output (AIE p.222-223).

## Structural Best Practices

- **Prompt structure:** A prompt generally has a task description, example(s), and the concrete task itself. Placement matters and is model-dependent: most models (including GPT-4) perform better with the task description at the beginning, while others (including Llama-3) perform better with it at the end (AIE p.212-213).
- **System vs. user prompts:** Splitting instructions (system prompt) from user-supplied data/questions (user prompt) is a common pattern, often used for roleplaying personas (AIE p.215).

## Other Techniques

- **Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting:** Explicitly asking the model to "think step by step" nudges it toward systematic problem solving, improves performance across benchmarks and model sizes, and has been found to reduce hallucinations — though it can increase perceived latency since multiple intermediate steps occur before the first output token (AIE p.227-229).
- **In-context learning / few-shot vs. zero-shot:** Teaching the model via examples in the prompt (shots) rather than updating weights, bounded by the model's maximum context length (AIE p.213).

## Overarching Principles

Prompt engineering should be treated with the same rigor as any ML experiment — systematic experimentation and evaluation are needed, and it should be exhausted before moving to more resource-intensive techniques like finetuning (AIE p.211-212). It is also recommended to version prompts and progressively add more examples (typically 1 to 50, depending on use case) before escalating to retrieval-augmented generation or finetuning when failures persist (AIE p.318).

## Q14 What are the malicious attacks that can be done with prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** The wiki identifies **three main types of prompt attacks** that application developers must defend against: [[prompt-extraction]], [[jailbreaking-and-prompt-injection]], and [[information-extraction]] (AIE p.235).

## 1. Prompt Extraction
A malicious user tries to extract an application's prompt, including its system prompt, either to replicate the application or to exploit it (AIE p.235).

## 2. Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection
These are treated together because they share the goal of getting a model to express undesirable behavior, using overlapping techniques (AIE p.238):

- **Jailbreaking**: subverting a model's safety features, e.g., getting a customer support bot to explain how to make a bomb (AIE p.238).
- **Prompt injection**: injecting malicious instructions into a prompt so the model executes them instead of, or alongside, the legitimate request, e.g., turning "When will my order arrive?" into a database-deletion command (AIE p.238).

Specific techniques include:
- **Direct manual prompt hacking**: hand-crafted, social-engineering-style prompts, including:
  - *Obfuscation* — misspelling blocked keywords or mixing languages/Unicode, or appending password-like special-character strings (AIE p.239).
  - *Output formatting manipulation* — hiding malicious intent in unexpected formats like poems, raps, code, or stylized text (AIE p.239-240).
  - *Roleplaying* — asking the model to pretend to be a character/scenario exempt from its rules, e.g. the [[dan-jailbreak]] ("Do Anything Now"), the grandma exploit, an NSA-agent persona, or "Filter Improvement Mode" (AIE p.240).
- **Automated attacks**: algorithms that partially or fully automate prompt hacking, such as random substring substitution (Zou et al., 2023) or the systematic AI-powered [[pair-jailbreak-method]], which often needs fewer than twenty queries (AIE p.240-241).
- **Indirect prompt injection**: placing malicious instructions in tools the model is integrated with, rather than directly in the user prompt. This includes:
  - *Passive phishing* — leaving malicious payloads in public spaces (web pages, GitHub repos, etc.) for a model to discover via tools like web search (AIE p.241-243).
  - *Active injection* — proactively sending a target malicious content, e.g., an email instructing an email-reading assistant to forward every email in the inbox, or a poisoned database username interpreted as a delete command (AIE p.241-243).

## 3. Information Extraction
Exploiting a language model's conversational interface to pull out training data or context information it was not meant to reveal (AIE p.243). Motivations include:
- **Data theft** — extracting training data to build a competitive model (AIE p.243).
- **Privacy violation** — exposing private data used in training or context (AIE p.243).
- **Copyright infringement** — getting the model to regurgitate copyrighted text (AIE p.243).

Techniques include fill-in-the-blank prompting to trigger memorized data (AIE p.244), and divergence attacks, such as prompting a model to repeat a word forever until it diverges into verbatim training data (Nasr et al., 2023) (AIE p.244). Extraction also applies beyond text, e.g., extracting near-duplicate images from diffusion models like Stable Diffusion (AIE p.245).

## Risks of These Attacks
Prompt attacks pose risks including (AIE p.235-236):
- Remote code or tool execution
- Data leaks
- Social harms (e.g., aiding weapon-making or tax evasion)
- Misinformation
- Service interruption and subversion
- Brand risk (reputational damage from offensive outputs)

See [[prompt-attack-risks]] for full detail.

## Q15 What is the future of comparative evaluation?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Comparative evaluation has a durable future, for three main reasons (AIE p.155):

1. **It's easier for humans to compare than to score.** It is easier for human evaluators to judge which of two outputs is better than to assign each a concrete score. As models surpass human performance, this may become the only viable form of human judgment: the [[llama-2]] paper reports that even when a model's writing exceeds the ability of the best human annotators, humans can still meaningfully compare two answers (Touvron et al., 2023) (AIE p.155).

2. **It targets what actually matters and never saturates.** Comparative evaluation measures human preference directly, which relieves the pressure to keep inventing new benchmarks to keep pace with expanding AI capability. Unlike benchmarks, which become useless once a model saturates them (see [[benchmark-saturation]]), comparative evaluation never saturates as long as newer, stronger models keep being introduced (AIE p.155).

3. **It's hard to game.** There is no easy way to cheat comparative evaluation the way one can train a model on reference data to inflate a benchmark score. This is why many trust public comparative leaderboards -- like [[lmsys-chatbot-arena]] -- more than other public leaderboards (AIE p.155).

Additionally, comparative evaluation can surface discriminating signals unavailable elsewhere: for offline evaluation it complements evaluation benchmarks, and for online evaluation it complements A/B testing (AIE p.156). This continuing need for preference signals is also what motivates the development of [[preference-model|preference models]], which supply comparison-based preference predictions automatically rather than requiring expensive human judgments (AIE p.156).

That said, the wiki also notes comparative evaluation's ongoing limitations -- challenges of scalability, standardization, and the fact that relative performance does not equal absolute performance -- which remain unresolved even as the technique's future use is expected to persist (AIE p.155).

## Q16 When should we finetune a model?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Finetuning should be considered only after prompt-based methods have been thoroughly tried, and generally sits as a later stage in a broader adaptation workflow rather than a first resort.

## Why finetuning is not the default first step

- Compared to prompt-based methods, finetuning requires significantly more resources — not just data and hardware, but ML talent — so it is generally attempted only after extensive experiments with prompting have already been tried (AIE p.311).
- Finetuning is rarely the right first step in a new project: it requires high up-front investment (annotated data, which is slow and expensive to acquire), the expertise to choose base models and tune training knobs, and ongoing serving/inference-optimization work once the model is finetuned (AIE p.313).
- It also requires an ongoing policy and budget for monitoring and maintenance, since new base models are released faster than a finetuned model can typically be improved (AIE p.313).
- Many complaints about prompting's ineffectiveness actually trace back to unclear instructions, unrepresentative examples, or poorly defined metrics — not a genuine limit of prompting — so the recommended path is to start with systematic prompting experiments and only move to finetuning (or other advanced solutions) once those prove inadequate (AIE p.314).

## When finetuning becomes the right call

The book frames the decision around *why* a model is failing (AIE p.316-317, [[finetuning-versus-rag]]):

- **Behavior-based failures** — outputs that are factually fine but irrelevant, malformatted, or stylistically wrong (e.g., HTML that doesn't compile, specs missing required detail) — call for finetuning. The summary rule given is: **"finetuning is for form, and RAG is for facts"** (AIE p.317).
- **Semantic parsing tasks**, which hinge on producing output in a specific structured format, often require finetuning because strong off-the-shelf models are weaker on less common syntaxes (AIE p.317).
- By contrast, **information-based failures** (factually wrong or outdated outputs due to missing or stale knowledge) are better addressed with RAG rather than finetuning (AIE p.316).

## Staged workflow

The book's suggested staged process ([[model-adaptation-workflow]]) is: (1) try prompting alone; (2) add more examples to the prompt (roughly 1–50); (3) if failures are information-based, add RAG, starting with basic term-based retrieval; (4) if information-based failures persist, move to more advanced RAG (e.g., embedding-based retrieval), while persistent *behavioral* issues call for finetuning; (5) optionally combine RAG and finetuning together for a further boost (AIE p.318-319).

## Specific reasons that motivate finetuning

- Improving general and task-specific quality, especially generating outputs in specific structures like JSON or YAML (AIE p.311).
- Closing gaps where a general-purpose model fails on a narrower task it wasn't sufficiently trained on (e.g., an uncommon SQL dialect or customer-specific queries) (AIE p.311).
- Bias mitigation — exposing a model to carefully curated data to counteract biases inherited from training data (AIE p.312).
- Using a small, finetuned model that outperforms a much larger out-of-the-box model on a specific task, which is cheaper and faster to run in production (AIE p.312).
- Historically, reducing token usage/cost versus repeating many examples in every prompt — though this benefit has "largely" been removed by the advent of prompt caching (AIE p.315).

## Caveat

Finetuning for one specific task can degrade performance on other tasks, so if finetuning is used, the model may need to be finetuned on the full mix of tasks it must handle, served as separate per-task models, or recombined via model merging (AIE p.312-313). Both prompting and finetuning experiments benefit from the same systematic processes: an evaluation pipeline, data-annotation guidelines, and experiment tracking (AIE p.314).

## Q18 What is the difference between data-centric AI and model-centric AI?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Model-centric AI improves performance by enhancing the model itself — designing new architectures, increasing [[model-size]], or developing new training techniques. Data-centric AI instead improves performance by enhancing the data — developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that let a better model be trained with fewer resources (AIE p.364).

This distinction is reflected in how deep learning benchmarks have evolved: early benchmarks like ImageNet were largely model-centric, with a fixed dataset and researchers competing to build the best model. More recent benchmarks, such as Andrew Ng's 2021 data-centric AI competition and DataComp (Gadre et al., 2023), flip this around — the model is fixed, and participants compete to build the best training dataset instead (AIE p.364).

Importantly, the book treats this as a framing device rather than a strict either/or choice: meaningful progress in practice usually requires investment in **both** model and data improvements, not a choice of one over the other (AIE p.365).

## Q20 How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related quantities that both measure properties of predictability and information content in language data, with cross-entropy building directly on entropy.

**The core relationship:**

A model's cross entropy on training data depends on two things: the training data's own entropy H(P), and how far the model's learned distribution Q diverges from the true distribution P, measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence D_KL(P||Q). This gives the formula:

H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q) (AIE p.120)

**What each measures:**

- Entropy measures how much information, on average, a token carries, and how difficult it is to predict what comes next in a language. The lower a language's entropy, the more predictable it is, since a token carries less information (AIE p.119).
- Cross entropy measures how difficult it is for a language model to predict what comes next in a dataset. When training on a dataset, the goal is to get the model to learn the true distribution of that data, and cross entropy quantifies how far the model's learned distribution is from succeeding at this (AIE p.120).

**How they converge:**

A language model is trained to minimize its cross entropy with respect to the training data. If it learns the training data's distribution perfectly, its cross entropy equals the training data's entropy, and the KL divergence term becomes 0. In this sense, cross entropy can be thought of as the model's approximation of the entropy of its training data (AIE p.121).

**Additional notes:**

- Cross entropy is not symmetric: H(P, Q) differs from H(Q, P) (AIE p.120).
- A language model encodes statistical information about how likely a token is to appear in a given context; the better it learns this distribution, the better it predicts what comes next in the training data, and the lower its training cross-entropy (AIE p.119).
- Entropy underlies cross-entropy, as well as perplexity, bits-per-character, and bits-per-byte — all four metrics are closely related, and knowing one lets you compute the other three given the necessary information (AIE p.119).
