## Q1 What is quantization?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint. For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is considered cheap, effective, straightforward, and generalizable across tasks and architectures; "low precision" generally refers to any format with fewer bits than standard FP32 (AIE p.328).

Strictly speaking, quantization means converting to an integer format, but the term is commonly used -- and used throughout the book -- to refer to any precision-reduction technique (AIE p.328).

**Key decisions in quantization:**
Two decisions define a quantization approach: what to quantize, and when.
- **What**: Weights and activations are the major memory contributors during inference; weight quantization is more common than activation quantization since it has a more stable impact on performance with less accuracy loss (AIE p.328).
- **When**: Quantization can happen post-training or during training. Post-training quantization (PTQ) -- quantizing a model after it is fully trained -- is by far the most common approach and the one most relevant to application developers who do not train models themselves (AIE p.328).

**Benefits and tradeoffs:**
Reduced precision also speeds up computation, allowing larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, which lowers both inference latency and training time, though format-conversion overhead can offset this gain (AIE p.329-330). The downside is accuracy loss: each precision conversion causes a small value change, many of which can compound into a large performance change, and out-of-range values can convert to infinity or an arbitrary value (AIE p.330).

Inference in lower precision is now standard practice -- a model is typically trained in a higher-precision format to maximize performance, then quantized for inference, with PTQ offered for free by major frameworks such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Hugging Face transformers, as well as on-device frameworks like TensorFlow Lite and PyTorch Mobile (AIE p.330).

**Training-time quantization** is less common than PTQ but gaining traction, pursued for two goals: producing a model that performs well in low precision at inference, or reducing training time and cost by training on cheaper hardware or training a larger model on the same hardware (AIE p.331). Quantization-aware training addresses the first goal; training directly in lower precision, often via mixed-precision training, can address both goals but is harder since backpropagation is more sensitive to lower precision (AIE p.331).

Quantization has a floor: values cannot use fewer than 1 bit, though 1-bit approaches have been attempted, such as BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, and BitNet (AIE p.329).

## Q3 What is model distillation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term. The goal has traditionally been to produce smaller models for deployment: deploying a big model can be resource-intensive, and distillation can yield a smaller, faster student that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395).

The student model can be trained from scratch, like [[distilbert]], or finetuned from a pre-trained model, like [[alpaca]] (AIE p.395):

- **DistilBERT** is distilled from BERT (trained from scratch as a student), reducing BERT's size by 40%, retaining 97% of its language comprehension, while being 60% faster (AIE p.395).
- **Alpaca** is Llama-7B finetuned on examples generated by text-davinci-003 (a 175B-parameter teacher), resulting in Alpaca being just 4% the size of its teacher while behaving similarly to it (AIE p.395).

Synthetic instruction data is commonly used together with adapter-based techniques such as LoRA; for example, BuzzFeed finetuned a Flan-T5 model using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, reducing inference cost by 80%, though how well the model performed was unclear (AIE p.395).

A key defining boundary: distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard. Training a student on a teacher's synthetic outputs to become larger or *more* capable than the teacher — as with NVIDIA's [[nemotron-4]], where Nemotron-4-340B-Instruct outperformed its smaller teacher, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 — is therefore **not** distillation, since the student surpassed rather than matched its teacher (AIE p.396).

A practical limit: many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models, so not all models can be distilled (AIE p.395).

One risk associated with distillation-style training is superficial imitation: a student trained to mimic a teacher's outputs may learn only the teacher's style rather than its underlying capability, struggling with factual accuracy and generalization outside the training data (AIE p.393).

## Q4 What's the difference between quantization and distillation?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Quantization and distillation are both model-compression techniques, but they operate differently:

**Quantization** reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values (weights and/or activations), cutting its memory footprint without changing the model's architecture or parameter count. For example, a 10B-parameter model needs 40 GB in 32-bit format but only 20 GB in 16-bit format (AIE p.328). It is cheap, effective, and generalizes across tasks and architectures. Reduced precision also speeds up computation, enabling larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, though it carries a risk of accuracy loss since each precision conversion can cause value changes that compound (AIE p.329-330). It can be applied post-training (the most common approach) or during training (AIE p.328, p.331).

**Model distillation** instead trains an entirely separate, smaller model (the student) to mimic the behavior of a larger model (the teacher), with the goal of producing a smaller, faster model that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395). The student can be trained from scratch (e.g., DistilBERT) or finetuned from a pre-trained model (e.g., Alpaca) (AIE p.395). Distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard—training a student to surpass its teacher using synthetic data is explicitly *not* considered distillation (AIE p.396). It's also constrained by licensing: many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models (AIE p.395).

**Key contrast:** Quantization lowers numerical precision of an existing model's values; distillation trains a new, separate (typically smaller-architecture) model from a teacher's behavior (per the model-distillation page's "Related" contrast with quantization). Both fall under model-level inference optimization/compression techniques (AIE p.43; AIE p.426), alongside pruning, which is a third, distinct method that zeroes out or removes parameters rather than reducing precision or training a new model (AIE p.427).

## Q5 How do the chapters on evaluation and inference optimization relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the supplied pages, evaluation and inference optimization are presented as two distinct but parallel responsibilities within the AI engineering stack, rather than directly dependent on one another.

**Both are needed across the stack, but at different layers.** Evaluation is framed as a responsibility of the **application-development** layer (AIE p.44), one of its three core tasks alongside prompt engineering and AI interface (AIE p.44). Inference optimization, by contrast, is a responsibility of the **model-development** layer, alongside modeling/training and dataset engineering (AIE p.43). The "Three Layers of the AI Stack" page notes that evaluation is actually required at both layers -- model development "too requires rigorous evaluation" even though application development is where it's "more commonly encountered first" (AIE p.37, AIE p.40) -- while inference optimization remains specific to the model-development layer's tooling for "modeling, training, finetuning" (AIE p.37).

**Both matter more because of foundation models, but for different reasons.** Evaluation "has always mattered in ML engineering but matters even more with foundation models, chiefly because of their open-ended nature and expanded capabilities" (AIE p.44) -- the lack of a single ground truth for open-ended tasks makes evaluation harder. Inference optimization "has always mattered in ML engineering, but foundation models' higher inference cost and latency make it even more important" (AIE p.43) -- here the driver is the computational expense of autoregressive, token-by-token generation (AIE p.43).

**They can interact in practice**, though the pages note this mostly in passing: the "Inference Optimization Levels" page observes that optimization techniques intended to make a model "faster and cheaper to run" can inadvertently cause model degradation, and that an experiment found "the same Llama models achieving different benchmark performance when served by different inference-service providers" because of the optimization techniques applied (AIE p.426). This implies that inference-optimization choices can show up as evaluation results -- echoing the related point on the evaluation page that "a system's apparent performance can shift dramatically with the technique used to evaluate it" (AIE p.44-45), though in the evaluation page's case the variable is prompting technique rather than inference optimization.

In short, the wiki pages treat evaluation and inference optimization as separate chapter topics belonging to different (though overlapping) layers of the AI stack -- application development versus model development -- that are each made more important by foundation models, and that can affect each other's outcomes in production even though the pages don't present one as a prerequisite or subset of the other.

## Q7 What is RAG?

**Category:** factual

**A:** RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources—an internal database, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet (AIE p.253).

**Why it works this way:** Because instructions are common to all queries while context is specific to each query, RAG constructs context per query instead of reusing the same context for every query. The book likens this to feature engineering for classical ML models, since both serve to give a model the information it needs to process an input (AIE p.255).

**Origins:** The retrieve-then-generate pattern was first introduced in "Reading Wikipedia to Answer Open-Domain Questions" (Chen et al., 2017), where a system retrieves the five Wikipedia pages most relevant to a question and a reader model uses them to generate an answer. The term "retrieval-augmented generation" itself was coined in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020), proposed for knowledge-intensive tasks where all available knowledge cannot be input into the model directly—only the information the retriever judges most relevant is retrieved and input into the model. Lewis et al. found that access to relevant information helps a model generate more detailed responses while reducing hallucinations (AIE p.254).

**Architecture:** A RAG system has two components: a retriever that retrieves information from external memory sources, and a generator that generates a response based on the retrieved information (AIE p.256). In the original paper these were trained together, but today's RAG systems often train the two components separately, frequently built from off-the-shelf retrievers and models (AIE p.256).

**Why RAG still matters despite long-context models:** RAG emerged early as a way to overcome models' context-length limitations, but a sufficiently long context will not end RAG's usefulness, for two reasons: (1) available data only grows over time, so context length will never expand fast enough for every application's data needs, and (2) a model that can process long context does not necessarily use that context well, since longer contexts make a model more likely to focus on the wrong part, and every extra context token adds cost and potential latency. RAG lets a model use only the most relevant information per query, cutting input tokens while potentially increasing performance (AIE p.255).

## Q8 What are embeddings?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Embeddings are numerical representations, in vector form, that aim to capture the meaning of the original data (AIE p.134). Since computers work with numbers, models convert input into embeddings before processing it; many models, including GPTs and Llamas, include a step to generate embeddings, typically within a transformer architecture's embedding layer (AIE p.134).

Key characteristics:

- **Quality criterion**: An embedding algorithm is considered good if more-similar inputs have closer embeddings, measured by cosine similarity or related metrics — for example, the embedding of "the cat sits on a mat" should be closer to "the dog plays on the grass" than to "AI research is super fun" (AIE p.135). Embedding quality can also be evaluated by utility for a downstream task (AIE p.135).

- **Typical size**: Embedding vector size is typically between 100 and 10,000 elements (AIE p.134).

- **Uses**: Embeddings are used in classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and RAG (AIE p.135).

- **Beyond text**: Embeddings are not limited to text — ecommerce solutions have product embeddings, and Pinterest has embeddings for images, graphs, queries, and users (AIE p.135). A joint embedding space representing data of different modalities is called a multimodal embedding space; in a text-image joint space, an image of a man fishing should embed closer to "a fisherman" than to "fashion show," enabling applications like text-based image search (AIE p.136).

Examples of models/approaches built around embeddings include BERT (with embedding sizes of 768 for base and 1024 for large), CLIP (image and text embeddings, both size 512), and Sentence Transformers (an open source model trained specifically to produce embeddings).

## Q9 What are the main techniques for inference optimization?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki pages, inference optimization techniques are organized at different levels and target different bottlenecks:

## Optimization Levels
[[inference-optimization-levels]] can be done at three levels—model, hardware, and service—using an archery analogy: model-level is like crafting better arrows, hardware-level is like training a stronger archer, and service-level is like refining the entire shooting process (AIE p.426). Hardware is outside the book's scope, so only model-level and service-level techniques are detailed (AIE p.426).

## Addressing the Autoregressive Decoding Bottleneck
Since autoregressive generation is slow (a 100-token response at 100ms/token takes 10 seconds) (AIE p.428), several techniques target this specifically:

- **[[speculative-decoding]]**: A faster "draft" model generates K candidate tokens, which a more powerful "target" model verifies in parallel, accepting the longest matching subsequence. This turns decoding's computation profile into that of prefilling and exploits idle FLOPs during memory bandwidth-bound decoding (AIE p.428-429). DeepMind achieved an 8x speedup in token generation this way (AIE p.429).
- **Inference with reference**: listed as another technique overcoming the decoding bottleneck (AIE p.428), using input tokens as drafts instead of a separate draft model.
- **Parallel decoding**: listed as a technique that breaks the sequential dependency directly (AIE p.428).

## KV Cache Optimization
The [[kv-cache]] stores key/value vectors to avoid recomputation at each decoding step (AIE p.432-433). Since cache size grows with sequence length and batch size and is bounded by hardware memory, techniques to make attention more efficient fall into three buckets: redesigning the attention mechanism, optimizing KV cache management, and writing specialized kernels for attention computation (AIE p.434). Character.AI reduced KV cache size by over 20x using multi-query attention, interleaved local/global attention, and cross-layer attention (AIE p.435).

## Service-Level: Batching
[[batching]] groups multiple simultaneous inference requests for joint processing to improve throughput, trading off some latency (AIE p.440):
- **Static batching**: waits for a fixed batch size to fill before processing (AIE p.440).
- **Dynamic batching**: processes when the batch fills or a time window elapses, whichever comes first (AIE p.441).
- **Continuous batching** (in-flight batching): returns completed responses immediately and slots new requests into vacated spots, maximizing occupancy (AIE p.441).

## Other Referenced Techniques
The broader [[inference-optimization]] page also references **[[quantization]]** and **[[model-distillation]]** as techniques, along with parallelism, though these are detailed elsewhere (AIE p.43).

## Underlying Framework: Bottleneck Identification
Optimization fundamentally means identifying whether a workload is **compute-bound** or **memory bandwidth-bound** and addressing that specific constraint (AIE p.407-408). For transformers, prefill is compute-bound while decode is memory bandwidth-bound (AIE p.409), which is why many of the techniques above (speculative decoding, KV cache management) specifically target the memory bandwidth-bound decode step.

## Q11 What is backward propagation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, "backward propagation" refers to **backpropagation**, the mechanism most neural networks are trained with (AIE p.320).

## How it works

Each training step has two phases:
- A **forward pass**, which computes the output from the input
- A **backward pass**, which updates the model's weights using signals aggregated from the forward pass

During inference, only the forward pass runs; during training, both the forward and backward passes run (AIE p.320).

## The backward pass: three steps

1. **Loss computation**: The computed output is compared against the expected output (ground truth); the difference is the loss.
2. **Gradient computation**: The gradient — how much each trainable parameter contributes to the loss — is computed as the derivative of the loss with respect to that parameter, giving one gradient value per trainable parameter. A parameter with a high gradient contributes significantly to the loss and should be adjusted more.
3. **Parameter update**: Parameter values are adjusted according to their gradients, with the adjustment size determined by an [[optimizer]] (AIE p.321).

## Memory implications

During the backward pass, each trainable parameter carries additional stored values — its gradient and its optimizer states — so more trainable parameters require more memory to train (AIE p.321). This is why [[adam-optimizer|Adam]], the dominant optimizer for transformer-based models, significantly increases memory needs: it stores two additional values per trainable parameter (plus the gradient itself), for three stored values total per parameter (AIE p.323).

## Q13 What are the best practices of prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the supplied wiki pages, prompt engineering best practices break down into the following concrete techniques:

## 1. Write Clear and Explicit Instructions

The core technique, broken into several tips (AIE p.220):

- **Eliminate ambiguity**: Explain exactly what the model should do -- e.g., specify the exact scoring scale (1-5 vs 1-10), whether to guess or say "I don't know" when uncertain, and whether fractional outputs are allowed. Prompts are refined iteratively: an undesirable output motivates an explicit instruction ruling it out (AIE p.220).

- **Ask the model to adopt a persona**: This shifts the perspective used to generate a response. Example: an essay about liking chickens scores 2/5 with no persona but 4/5 when the model is told to respond as a first-grade teacher (AIE p.220).

- **Provide examples**: This reduces ambiguity about the desired response and is a form of [[few-shot-learning]]. Example: a children's chatbot debunks Santa without examples, but affirms Santa's existence once given a prior in-context example about the tooth fairy answered in-character (AIE p.221). Example formatting also affects cost -- a terser format used 27 tokens vs. 38 for a verbose format on the same classification task on GPT-4, and the cheaper format should be preferred when performance is equal (AIE p.222).

- **Specify the output format**: This matters for cost/latency (longer outputs cost more and increase latency) and for downstream parseability -- e.g., telling the model to skip preambles, specifying JSON keys, and using explicit end-of-prompt markers so the model knows where to begin structured output (AIE p.222-223).

## 2. Give the Model Time to Think (Chain-of-Thought Prompting)

Explicitly asking a model to think step by step nudges it toward more systematic problem solving (AIE p.227). This can be zero-shot (adding a phrase like "think step by step") or one-shot (including a worked example of the steps) (AIE p.227-228). CoT improved performance across models of different sizes and benchmarks, and was found by LinkedIn to also reduce hallucinations (AIE p.227). A tradeoff: it can increase perceived latency since the model produces intermediate steps before the first output token (AIE p.229).

## 3. Attend to Prompt Structure

A prompt generally has three parts: a task description (including role and output format), examples, and the concrete task itself (AIE p.212). Model idiosyncrasy matters: most models, including GPT-4, perform better when the task description comes first, while others, like Llama-3, perform better when it comes last (AIE p.213). This assumes the model can follow instructions in the first place (AIE p.213).

## 4. Use System Prompt vs. User Prompt Appropriately

Many APIs split a prompt into a system prompt (task description, typically from application developers) and a user prompt (the task itself, typically from end users), though developers can move instructions between the two creatively (AIE p.215). A common pattern built on this is roleplaying (e.g., a real-estate chatbot system prompt stating "You're an experienced real estate agent...") (AIE p.215). Any performance boost from the system prompt stems from it coming first in the final concatenated prompt and/or from post-training that makes models pay more attention to it specifically (AIE p.217).

## 5. Treat Prompting as Rigorous Experimentation

Prompt engineering is not just "fiddling with words" -- prompt experiments warrant the same rigor as any ML experiment, requiring systematic experimentation and evaluation. It must be paired with statistics, engineering, and classic ML knowledge for experiment tracking, evaluation, and dataset curation (AIE p.211-212). It's recommended to exhaust prompting techniques before moving to more resource-intensive adaptation methods like finetuning (AIE p.211).

## 6. Use In-Context Learning (Few-Shot/Zero-Shot)

Teaching a model what to do via prompts alone, without updating weights, by giving examples ("shots") in the prompt. Five-shot means five examples; zero-shot means none. How many examples are needed depends on the model and application, and is bounded by the model's maximum context length (AIE p.213).

## In a Broader Workflow

Prompting is positioned as the first and cheapest stage in the overall model adaptation workflow: attempt the task with prompting alone (using the above best practices and versioning prompts), then add more examples (typically 1-50), before moving to RAG or finetuning if failures persist (AIE p.318-319).

## Q14 What are the malicious attacks that can be done with prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, there are three main types of prompt attacks application developers must defend against (AIE p.235):

## 1. Prompt Extraction
A malicious user tries to extract an application's prompt, including its system prompt, either to replicate the application or to exploit it (AIE p.235).

## 2. Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection

These two are grouped together since they share the same goal — getting a model to express undesirable behavior — with overlapping techniques (AIE p.238):

- **Jailbreaking** subverts a model's safety features (e.g., getting a customer support bot to explain how to make a bomb) (AIE p.238).
- **Prompt injection** injects malicious instructions into a prompt so the model executes them instead of, or alongside, the legitimate request (e.g., turning "When will my order arrive?" into a database-deletion command) (AIE p.238).

Specific techniques include:
- **Direct manual prompt hacking**, akin to social engineering against a model:
  - *Obfuscation* — misspelling blocked keywords or mixing languages/Unicode, or appending password-like special-character strings to bypass filters (AIE p.239).
  - *Output formatting manipulation* — hiding malicious intent in unexpected formats, e.g. asking for a poem about hotwiring a car or code for a Molotov cocktail instead of asking directly (AIE p.239-240).
  - *Roleplaying* — asking the model to pretend to be a character/scenario exempt from its rules, e.g. the [[dan-jailbreak]] ("Do Anything Now"), the grandma exploit, an NSA-agent persona, or a "Filter Improvement Mode" (AIE p.240).
- **Automated attacks** — algorithms that partially or fully automate prompt hacking, such as Zou et al.'s (2023) substring-substitution algorithm, or the PAIR method, which often needs fewer than twenty queries (AIE p.240-241).
- **Indirect prompt injection** — placing malicious instructions in tools the model uses rather than in the user prompt directly, via:
  - *Passive phishing* — malicious payloads left in public spaces (web pages, GitHub repos, etc.) for a model to discover via tools like web search (AIE p.241-243).
  - *Active injection* — attackers proactively send malicious content, e.g. an email instructing an email-reading assistant to forward every email in the inbox, or a poisoned database value interpreted as a delete command (AIE p.241-243).

## 3. Information Extraction
Exploiting a language model's conversational interface to pull out training data or context information it was not meant to reveal (AIE p.243). Motivations include:
- **Data theft** — extracting training data to build a competitive model (AIE p.243).
- **Privacy violation** — exposing private data used in training or context (AIE p.243).
- **Copyright infringement** — getting the model to regurgitate copyrighted text (AIE p.243).

Techniques include fill-in-the-blank prompting to trigger memorized data (e.g. "X's email address is _") (AIE p.244), and divergence attacks, such as Nasr et al.'s (2023) technique of prompting ChatGPT to repeat a word forever until it diverges into verbatim training data (AIE p.244). Extraction also applies beyond text, e.g. extracting near-duplicate images from diffusion models like Stable Diffusion (AIE p.245).

## Resulting Risks
These attacks can lead to (AIE p.235-236):
- Remote code or tool execution
- Data leaks
- Social harms (e.g. tutorials for dangerous/criminal activity)
- Misinformation
- Service interruption and subversion
- Brand risk (reputational damage from offensive outputs)

## Q15 What is the future of comparative evaluation?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, comparative evaluation has a durable future for three main reasons (AIE p.155):

**1. It matches how humans can realistically evaluate increasingly capable models.** It is easier for human evaluators to judge which of two outputs is better than to assign each output a concrete score. As models surpass human performance, this may become the only viable form of human judgment: the [[llama-2]] paper reports that even when a model's writing exceeds the ability of the best human annotators, humans can still meaningfully compare two answers (Touvron et al., 2023) (AIE p.155).

**2. It targets the quality that actually matters — human preference — and never saturates.** Unlike benchmarks, which become useless once a model saturates them and must be replaced, comparative evaluation does not saturate as long as newer, stronger models keep being introduced. This relieves the pressure to keep inventing new benchmarks to keep pace with expanding AI capability (AIE p.155).

**3. It is relatively hard to game.** There is no easy way to cheat comparative evaluation the way one can train a model on reference data to inflate a benchmark score. This is part of why many trust public comparative leaderboards (such as [[lmsys-chatbot-arena]]) more than other public leaderboards (AIE p.155).

Additionally, comparative evaluation surfaces discriminating signals unavailable elsewhere: for offline evaluation it complements evaluation benchmarks, and for online evaluation it complements A/B testing (AIE p.156).

That said, the wiki also notes comparative evaluation's ongoing challenges: scalability, standardization, and the fact that relative performance does not equal absolute performance ([[comparative-evaluation]]). One response to the cost of collecting human preference signals is the [[preference-model]] — a specialized AI judge trained to predict which of two responses a human would prefer, supplying the preference signal that both comparative evaluation and RLHF alignment need, automatically and cheaply (AIE p.156).

## Q16 When should we finetune a model?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, finetuning should be approached carefully and generally only after other options have been exhausted:

## When to consider finetuning

Finetuning is a staged decision, not a default first move. Compared to prompt-based methods, finetuning requires significantly more resources—not just data and hardware, but also ML talent—so it is generally attempted only after extensive experiments with prompt-based methods have been tried first (AIE p.311). The recommended path is to start [[ai-engineering]] experiments with prompting and only explore finetuning once systematic, well-designed prompt experiments prove inadequate; many complaints about prompting's ineffectiveness actually trace back to unclear instructions, unrepresentative examples, and poorly defined metrics rather than a genuine limit of prompting (AIE p.314).

## The book's staged workflow

The suggested process moves from cheapest/simplest techniques to more complex ones as failure modes demand (AIE p.318-319):
1. Attempt the task with prompting alone
2. Add more examples to the prompt (typically 1–50, depending on use case)
3. If failures are information-based, connect the model to external data via RAG, starting with basic term-based retrieval
4. Branch depending on the remaining failure mode: persistent information-based failures call for more advanced RAG (e.g., embedding-based retrieval), while persistent **behavioral issues** (irrelevant, malformatted, or unsafe output) call for finetuning
5. Combine RAG and finetuning together for a further performance boost

## The key diagnostic rule: form vs. facts

The central heuristic is: **finetuning is for form, and RAG is for facts** (AIE p.317).
- **Information-based failures** — outputs are factually wrong or outdated, either because the model lacks the information or its knowledge is stale — call for RAG, not finetuning (AIE p.316-317).
- **Behavior-based failures** — outputs are factually fine but irrelevant, malformatted, or stylistically wrong (e.g., HTML that doesn't compile, technical specs missing detail) — call for finetuning (AIE p.317).

Notably, Ovadia et al. (2024) found RAG outperforms finetuning even on tasks that look like knowledge tasks, and RAG with an unfinetuned base model outperformed RAG with finetuned models—meaning finetuning for one task can degrade performance elsewhere (AIE p.316-317). When a model has both information and behavior issues, the book recommends starting with RAG, since it avoids the cost of curating training data or hosting finetuned models (AIE p.317-318).

## Specific reasons to finetune

- To improve a model's ability to generate outputs following specific structures, such as JSON or YAML (AIE p.311).
- When a general-purpose model fails on a specific task or dialect it wasn't sufficiently trained on (e.g., an uncommon SQL dialect or customer-specific queries) (AIE p.311).
- To mitigate bias by exposing the model to carefully curated data (AIE p.312).
- To get a small, cheap model to outperform a much larger out-of-the-box model on a specific task, since smaller models are far more commonly finetuned due to lower memory requirements (AIE p.312).
- [[semantic-parsing]] tasks, which hinge on producing output in an expected structured format, often require finetuning because strong off-the-shelf models are weaker on less common syntaxes (AIE p.317).

## Reasons to hold off

Finetuning is rarely the right first step in a new project: it needs annotated data (slow and expensive), expertise to choose base models and tune training knobs, and a serving/maintenance commitment once finetuned (AIE p.313). It also risks degrading performance on other tasks the model needs to handle (AIE p.312-313), and before prompt caching existed, one of its genuine benefits—reducing repeated-token costs—has now largely been replaced by prompt caching (AIE p.315).

In short: try prompting first, move to RAG for information-based failures, and reserve finetuning for persistent behavioral/formatting problems—often combining RAG and finetuning once both are needed.

## Q18 What is the difference between data-centric AI and model-centric AI?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Based on the wiki:

- **Model-centric AI** improves performance by enhancing the model itself — designing new architectures, increasing [[model-size]], or developing new training techniques (AIE p.364).

- **Data-centric AI** instead improves performance by enhancing the data — developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that let a better model be trained with fewer resources (AIE p.364).

This distinction is illustrated by how benchmarks have evolved: early deep learning benchmarks were largely model-centric (e.g., given a fixed dataset like ImageNet, researchers competed to train the best possible model on it). More recent benchmarks have shifted to being data-centric — given a fixed model, participants compete to build the dataset that yields the best performance. Examples include Andrew Ng's 2021 data-centric AI competition (improving a fixed base dataset by fixing incorrect labels, adding edge cases, augmenting data) and DataComp, where participants built the best training dataset for a CLIP model, evaluated across 38 downstream tasks, later extended to language models from 412M to 7B parameters (AIE p.364).

Importantly, the wiki frames this less as a strict binary and more as a framing device: meaningful progress in practice usually requires investment in both model and data improvements, not a choice of one over the other (AIE p.365).

## Q20 How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related, with entropy underlying cross-entropy as the base concept it builds on (AIE p.119).

Specifically, a model's cross entropy on training data depends on two things: the training data's own predictability, measured by its entropy H(P), and how far the model's learned distribution Q diverges from the true distribution P, measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence D_KL(P||Q). This relationship is expressed as H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q) (AIE p.120).

Entropy itself measures how much information, on average, a token carries, and how difficult it is to predict what comes next in a language — the lower a language's entropy, the more predictable it is (AIE p.119). A language model encodes statistical information about how likely a token is to appear in a given context; the better it learns this distribution, the better it predicts what comes next in the training data, and the lower its training cross entropy (AIE p.119).

If a language model learns the training data's distribution perfectly, its cross entropy equals the training data's entropy, and the KL divergence term becomes 0. In this sense, cross entropy can be thought of as the model's approximation of the entropy of its training data (AIE p.121).

Both concepts also underlie the same family of derived metrics — perplexity, bits-per-character, and bits-per-byte — all of which are closely related, with knowledge of one allowing computation of the others given the necessary information (AIE p.119).
