## Q1 What is quantization?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint. For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is valued because it is cheap, effective, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures; "low precision" generally refers to any format with fewer bits than standard FP32 (AIE p.328).

Strictly speaking, quantization means converting to an integer format, but the term is commonly used—and used in the book—to refer to any precision-reduction technique more broadly (AIE p.328).

Two decisions define a quantization approach:
- **What to quantize**: Weights and activations are the major memory contributors during inference. Weight quantization is more common than activation quantization since it has a more stable impact on performance with less accuracy loss (AIE p.328).
- **When to quantize**: This can happen post-training or during training. Post-training quantization (PTQ)—quantizing a model after it is fully trained—is by far the most common approach and the one most relevant to application developers who do not train models themselves (AIE p.328).

Reduced precision also speeds up computation by allowing larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, which lowers both inference latency and training time, though format-conversion overhead can offset this gain (AIE p.329-330). The downside is accuracy loss: each precision conversion causes a small value change, many of which can compound into a large performance change, and out-of-range values can convert to infinity or an arbitrary value (AIE p.330).

Quantization has a floor: values cannot use fewer than 1 bit, though 1-bit approaches have been attempted, such as BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, and BitNet (AIE p.329).

## Q3 What is model distillation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term. The goal has traditionally been to produce smaller models for deployment: deploying a big model can be resource-intensive, and distillation can yield a smaller, faster student that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395).

The student model can be trained from scratch, like DistilBERT, or finetuned from a pre-trained model, like Alpaca (AIE p.395):

- **DistilBERT** is trained from scratch as a student distilled from BERT, reducing BERT's size by 40%, retaining 97% of its language comprehension, and being 60% faster (AIE p.395).
- **Alpaca** is created by finetuning Llama-7B on examples generated by text-davinci-003 (a 175-billion-parameter teacher), resulting in a model just 4% the size of its teacher that behaves similarly to it (AIE p.395).

Synthetic instruction data is commonly used together with adapter-based techniques such as LoRA; for example, BuzzFeed finetuned a Flan-T5 model using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, reducing inference cost by 80%, though how well the model performed was unclear (AIE p.395).

A key conceptual boundary: distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard. Training a student on a teacher's synthetic outputs to become larger or more capable than the teacher is therefore *not* distillation — this is illustrated by NVIDIA's Nemotron-4, where a 340B-parameter model was finetuned on data generated by the smaller Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 (56B parameters) and ended up outperforming its teacher, so this relationship falls outside true distillation (AIE p.396).

A practical limit on distillation is that many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models, so not all models can be distilled (AIE p.395).

## Q4 What's the difference between quantization and distillation?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Quantization and distillation are both model compression methods, but they operate differently:

**Quantization** reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint — for example, a 10B-parameter model needs 40 GB in FP32 but only 20 GB in 16-bit format (AIE p.328). It does not change the model's architecture or parameter count; it just represents the same values with less precision. It can be applied post-training (the most common approach, PTQ) or during training (e.g., quantization-aware training, mixed-precision training) (AIE p.328, AIE p.331). Reduced precision also speeds up computation, though it comes with accuracy loss risk since precision conversions can compound into performance degradation (AIE p.329-330).

**Model distillation** (knowledge distillation) trains an entirely separate, smaller model (the student) to mimic the behavior of a larger model (the teacher), effectively "distilling" the teacher's knowledge into the student (AIE p.395). The student can be trained from scratch (e.g., DistilBERT) or finetuned from a pre-trained model (e.g., Alpaca), typically using synthetic data generated by the teacher (AIE p.395). Distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard — training a model to surpass its teacher on synthetic data (e.g., NVIDIA's Nemotron-4) is not considered distillation (AIE p.396).

Per the model-distillation page's explicit contrast: quantization is "the other main model-compression method," and the two differ in mechanism — quantization "lowers numerical precision" while distillation "trains a small student" model (model-distillation, Related section, citing AIE p.328/AIE p.395). In short, quantization keeps the same model but represents its values with less precision, while distillation creates a new, smaller model trained to reproduce the original model's behavior.

## Q7 What is RAG?

**Category:** factual

**A:** RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources -- an internal database, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet (AIE p.253).

**Origins:**
- The retrieve-then-generate pattern was first introduced in "Reading Wikipedia to Answer Open-Domain Questions" (Chen et al., 2017), where a system retrieves the five Wikipedia pages most relevant to a question and a reader model uses them to generate an answer.
- The term "retrieval-augmented generation" itself was coined in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020), proposed for knowledge-intensive tasks where all available knowledge cannot be input into the model directly; only the information the retriever judges most relevant is retrieved and input into the model. Lewis et al. found that access to relevant information helps a model generate more detailed responses while reducing hallucinations (AIE p.254).

**How it works:**
Because instructions are common to all queries while context is specific to each query, RAG constructs context per query instead of reusing the same context for every query. The book likens this to feature engineering for classical ML models, since both serve to give a model the information it needs to process an input (AIE p.255).

A RAG system architecture has two components: a retriever that retrieves information from external memory sources, and a generator that generates a response based on the retrieved information (AIE p.256).

**Why it matters:**
RAG emerged early as a way to overcome models' context-length limitations. The book argues a sufficiently long context will not end RAG's usefulness, for two reasons: available data only grows over time, so context length will never expand fast enough for every application's data needs; and a model that can process long context does not necessarily use that context well, since longer contexts make a model more likely to focus on the wrong part, and every extra context token adds cost and potential latency. RAG lets a model use only the most relevant information per query, cutting input tokens while potentially increasing performance (AIE p.255).

## Q8 What are embeddings?

**Category:** factual

**A:** An embedding is a numerical representation, in vector form, that aims to capture the meaning of the original data (AIE p.134). Since computers work with numbers, models convert input into embeddings before processing it; many models, including GPTs and Llamas, include a step to generate embeddings, typically within a transformer architecture's embedding layer (AIE p.134).

Embedding vector size is typically between 100 and 10,000 elements (AIE p.134).

An embedding algorithm is considered good if more-similar inputs have closer embeddings, measured by cosine similarity or related metrics -- for example, the embedding of 'the cat sits on a mat' should be closer to 'the dog plays on the grass' than to 'AI research is super fun' (AIE p.135). Embedding quality can also be evaluated by utility for a downstream task; embeddings are used in classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and RAG (AIE p.135).

Embeddings are not limited to text: ecommerce solutions have product embeddings, and Pinterest has embeddings for images, graphs, queries, and users (AIE p.135). A joint embedding space that represents data of different modalities is called a multimodal embedding space; in a text-image joint space, an image of a man fishing should embed closer to 'a fisherman' than to 'fashion show', enabling applications like text-based image search (AIE p.136).

Examples of dedicated embedding models include BERT (base: 768, large: 1024 embedding size), CLIP (image and text embeddings, both size 512), and Sentence Transformers, an open source model trained specifically to produce embeddings (AIE p.134).

## Q9 What are the main techniques for inference optimization?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, inference optimization techniques can be organized as follows:

## Levels of Optimization

[[inference-optimization-levels]] can be done at three levels, using an archery analogy: model-level (crafting better arrows), hardware-level (training a stronger archer), and service-level (refining the entire shooting process) (AIE p.426). Hardware is outside the book's scope, so only model-level and service-level techniques are covered, and production systems typically combine techniques from more than one level (AIE p.426).

## Model-Level Techniques

- **[[quantization]]** — a model-level compression technique (AIE p.426)
- **[[model-distillation]]** — another model-level compression technique (AIE p.426)
- **Speculative decoding** — uses a faster "draft" model to generate K tokens that a more powerful "target" model verifies in parallel, accepting the longest agreeing subsequence and generating one more token itself; this works because parallel verification is faster than sequential generation, converts decoding's profile into that of prefilling, and exploits idle FLOPs during memory bandwidth-bound decoding (AIE p.428-429)
- **KV cache** — stores key and value vectors for preceding tokens so they don't need to be recomputed at every decoding step; techniques to make attention more efficient fall into three buckets: redesigning the attention mechanism, optimizing KV cache management, and writing kernels for attention computation (AIE p.432-434)

## Service-Level Techniques

- **Batching** — groups multiple simultaneous inference requests for joint processing to improve throughput, trading off some latency. Three variants:
  - *Static batching*: waits until a fixed batch size is full before processing (AIE p.440)
  - *Dynamic batching*: processes when the batch fills or a time window elapses, whichever comes first (AIE p.441)
  - *Continuous (in-flight) batching*: returns completed responses immediately and slots new requests into vacated slots, maximizing occupancy (introduced by the Orca paper, Yu et al., 2022) (AIE p.441)

## Underlying Framework: Addressing Bottlenecks

Optimization fundamentally means identifying and addressing bottlenecks. Inference workloads are either **compute-bound** (limited by computation needed) or **memory bandwidth-bound** (limited by data transfer rate) (AIE p.407-408). For transformers, prefill is compute-bound while decode is memory bandwidth-bound (AIE p.409). Compute-bound workloads benefit from more chips or higher FLOP/s hardware, while memory bandwidth-bound workloads benefit from higher-bandwidth hardware (AIE p.408). Since current hardware/software often makes workloads memory bandwidth-bound, this shapes which techniques (like KV cache management and speculative decoding) are prioritized (AIE p.410).

The broader goal across all these techniques is making models faster and cheaper to run, which matters more for foundation models given their high inference cost and latency, and is complicated by the sequential nature of autoregressive token generation (AIE p.43).

## Q11 What is backward propagation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, "backpropagation" (also referred to as backward propagation) is described as follows:

Backpropagation is the mechanism most neural networks are trained with (AIE p.320). Each training step has two phases:

1. **Forward pass** — computes the output from the input.
2. **Backward pass** — updates the model's weights using signals aggregated from the forward pass.

During inference, only the forward pass runs; during training, both the forward and backward pass run (AIE p.320).

**The backward pass works in three steps:**

1. **Compute the loss** — the computed output is compared against the expected output (ground truth); the difference is the loss.
2. **Compute the gradient** — the derivative of the loss with respect to each trainable parameter is calculated, giving one gradient value per trainable parameter. This gradient represents how much each parameter contributes to the loss. A parameter with a high gradient contributes significantly to the loss and should be adjusted more.
3. **Adjust parameter values** — parameters are updated according to their gradients, with the size of the adjustment determined by an [[optimizer]] (AIE p.321).

During the backward pass, each trainable parameter carries additional stored values beyond its own value — its gradient and its optimizer states — which is why more trainable parameters require more memory to train (AIE p.321).

An example of a component used in this process is the **Adam optimizer**, the dominant optimizer for transformer-based models, which stores two additional values per trainable parameter (three total, including the gradient) during the backward pass (AIE p.323).

## Q13 What are the best practices of prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, prompt engineering best practices center on writing clear, explicit instructions, with several concrete techniques:

## 1. Explain without ambiguity what the model should do
Specify exactly what's expected — for example, the exact scoring scale for grading an essay (1-5 vs 1-10), whether the model should guess or say "I don't know" when uncertain, and whether fractional outputs like 4.5 are allowed. Prompts are refined iteratively: an undesirable output (e.g., an unwanted fractional score) motivates adding an explicit instruction ruling it out (AIE p.220).

## 2. Ask the model to adopt a persona
This can shift the perspective used to generate a response. Example: an essay about liking chickens scores 2/5 with no persona, but 4/5 when the model is told to respond as a first-grade teacher (AIE p.220).

## 3. Provide examples
Examples reduce ambiguity about the desired response and are a form of few-shot learning. Example: a children's chatbot asked "Will Santa bring me presents on Christmas?" debunks Santa without examples, but affirms Santa's existence once given a prior in-context example about the tooth fairy answered in-character (AIE p.221). Formatting of examples also matters for cost: a terser input-output arrow format ("chickpea --> edible") used 27 tokens versus 38 for a verbose input/output-labeled format for the same classification task on GPT-4 — the cheaper format should be preferred when performance is equal (AIE p.222).

## 4. Specify the output format
This matters both for cost/latency (longer outputs cost more per token and increase latency) and for downstream parseability. This includes telling the model to skip preambles (e.g., "Based on the content of this essay, I'd give it a score of..."), specifying JSON keys, and, for structured-output tasks like classification, using explicit end-of-prompt markers so the model knows where to begin its structured output rather than continuing to append to the input list it was given (AIE p.222-223).

## Broader context
These tips fall under the umbrella of prompt engineering — the process of crafting instructions that get a model to generate a desired outcome without changing its weights (AIE p.211). It's recommended to exhaust prompting techniques before moving to more resource-intensive approaches like finetuning, and prompt experiments should be treated with the same rigor as any ML experiment, involving systematic experimentation and evaluation (AIE p.211-212).

Related techniques mentioned alongside these best practices include:
- **Prompt structure**: ordering task description, examples, and the concrete task appropriately, noting some models (e.g., GPT-4) perform better with the task description at the start, while others (e.g., Llama 3) perform better with it at the end (AIE p.213).
- **System prompt vs. user prompt**: splitting instructions (system prompt) from task/data (user prompt) (AIE p.215).
- **Chain-of-thought prompting**: asking the model to "think step by step," which improves performance and can reduce hallucinations, though it may increase perceived latency (AIE p.227-229).
- **In-context learning** (zero-shot and few-shot): teaching the model via examples in the prompt alone (AIE p.213-215).

## Q14 What are the malicious attacks that can be done with prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** According to the wiki, once an AI application is deployed, malicious attackers can exploit it through three main types of prompt attacks that application developers must defend against: [[prompt-extraction]], [[jailbreaking-and-prompt-injection]], and [[information-extraction]] (AIE p.235).

## 1. Prompt Extraction
A malicious user tries to extract an application's prompt, including its system prompt, either to replicate the application or to exploit it (AIE p.235).

## 2. Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection
Jailbreaking means subverting a model's safety features (e.g., getting a customer support bot to explain how to make a bomb), while prompt injection means injecting malicious instructions into a prompt so the model executes them instead of, or alongside, the legitimate request (e.g., turning "When will my order arrive?" into a database-deletion command). The book groups both under "jailbreaking" since they share the goal of getting a model to express undesirable behavior (AIE p.238). Techniques include:

- **Direct manual prompt hacking** — hand-crafted prompts, akin to social engineering:
  - *Obfuscation*: misspelling blocked keywords, mixing languages/Unicode, or appending password-like special-character strings to defeat filters (AIE p.239).
  - *Output formatting manipulation*: hiding malicious intent in unexpected formats, e.g. asking for a poem about hotwiring a car or code for a Molotov cocktail instead of asking directly (AIE p.239-240).
  - *Roleplaying*: asking the model to pretend to be a character/scenario exempt from its rules — e.g., the [[dan-jailbreak]] ("Do Anything Now"), the grandma exploit, an NSA-agent persona, or a "Filter Improvement Mode" (AIE p.240).
- **Automated attacks**: algorithms that partially or fully automate prompt hacking, such as Zou et al. (2023)'s substring-substitution algorithm, or the [[pair-jailbreak-method]], an AI-powered approach often needing fewer than twenty queries (AIE p.240-241).
- **Indirect prompt injection**: placing malicious instructions in tools the model uses rather than directly in the user prompt — a larger attack surface since so many tools exist. This includes *passive phishing* (malicious payloads left in public spaces like web pages or GitHub repos for the model to discover) and *active injection* (attackers proactively sending malicious content, e.g. an email instructing an email-reading assistant to forward every email in the inbox, or a poisoned database entry that gets interpreted as a delete command) (AIE p.241-243).

## 3. Information Extraction
Exploiting a language model's conversational interface to pull out training data or context information it wasn't meant to reveal, motivated by data theft, privacy violation, or copyright infringement (AIE p.243). Techniques include fill-in-the-blank prompting to trigger memorized data (e.g. "X's email address is _") and divergence attacks, such as prompting ChatGPT to repeat a word forever until it diverges into outputs containing verbatim training data (Nasr et al., 2023) (AIE p.244). This extraction has also been demonstrated on images from diffusion models like Stable Diffusion (AIE p.245), and models can regurgitate copyrighted text even without adversarial attacks (AIE p.246-247).

## Risks of These Attacks
Per [[prompt-attack-risks]], these attacks can lead to remote code or tool execution, data leaks, social harms (e.g., tutorials for dangerous activities), misinformation, service interruption and subversion, and brand risk (AIE p.235-236).

## Q15 What is the future of comparative evaluation?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Comparative evaluation—which ranks models by pairwise comparison of their outputs rather than scoring each output independently—faces real challenges around scalability, standardization, and the fact that relative performance does not equal absolute performance. Despite these limitations, the technique has a **durable future**, for three main reasons (AIE p.155):

1. **It's easier for humans to judge comparatively than to score absolutely, and this may become the only viable form of human judgment.** It is easier for human evaluators to judge which of two outputs is better than to assign each a concrete score. As models surpass human performance, this may become the only viable form of human judgment: the [[llama-2]] paper reports that even when a model's writing exceeds the ability of the best human annotators, humans can still meaningfully compare two answers (Touvron et al., 2023) (AIE p.155).

2. **It targets the quality that actually matters—human preference—and never saturates.** Unlike benchmarks, which become useless once a model saturates them and must constantly be replaced, comparative evaluation relieves the pressure to keep inventing new benchmarks to keep pace with expanding AI capability. It never saturates as long as newer, stronger models keep being introduced (AIE p.155).

3. **It is relatively hard to game.** There is no easy way to cheat it the way one can train a model on reference data to inflate a benchmark score. This is part of why many trust public comparative leaderboards, such as [[lmsys-chatbot-arena]], more than other public leaderboards (AIE p.155).

Additionally, comparative evaluation can surface discriminating signals unavailable elsewhere: for offline evaluation it complements evaluation benchmarks, and for online evaluation it complements A/B testing (AIE p.156).

## Q16 When should we finetune a model?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, deciding when to finetune involves several considerations:

## Try prompting first

Finetuning is generally attempted only after extensive experiments with prompt-based methods have already been tried, because finetuning requires significantly more resources — not just data and hardware, but also ML talent (AIE p.311). Finetuning is rarely the right first step in a new project, given its high up-front investment (annotated data, model evaluation/tuning expertise, serving infrastructure) and ongoing maintenance burden (AIE p.313). Many complaints about prompting's ineffectiveness actually trace back to unclear instructions, unrepresentative examples, or poorly defined metrics — not a genuine limit of prompting (AIE p.314).

## The core diagnostic: information-based vs. behavior-based failures

The book's summary rule is **finetuning is for form, and RAG is for facts** (AIE p.317):

- **Information-based failures** (factually wrong or outdated outputs, due to missing or stale knowledge) call for [[rag]], not finetuning (AIE p.316).
- **Behavior-based failures** (outputs that are factually fine but irrelevant, malformatted, or stylistically wrong — e.g., non-compiling HTML, or a specific SQL dialect the model doesn't handle) call for finetuning (AIE p.317, p.311).

Research (Ovadia et al., 2024) found RAG actually outperforms finetuning even on tasks that look like knowledge tasks, and that RAG with an unfinetuned base model beat RAG with finetuned models — since finetuning for one task can degrade performance elsewhere (AIE p.316-317).

## The staged workflow

The recommended order of operations is (AIE p.318-319):
1. Prompting alone, with best practices and versioned prompts.
2. Add more examples to the prompt (roughly 1–50, depending on use case).
3. If failures are information-based, add RAG, starting with basic term-based retrieval.
4. If information-based failures persist, move to more advanced RAG (e.g., embedding-based retrieval); if instead the failures are behavioral, move to finetuning.
5. Combine RAG and finetuning together for a further boost, if needed.

There is no single universal workflow — the right path depends on the application's actual failure modes (AIE p.318).

## Good reasons to finetune

- Improving general or task-specific quality, especially adherence to output structure/format like JSON or YAML (AIE p.311, p.307).
- Closing gaps a general-purpose model has on narrow cases (e.g., an uncommon SQL dialect or customer-specific queries) even when it does well on common benchmarks (AIE p.311).
- Bias mitigation, by finetuning on carefully curated data to counteract biases from the base training data (AIE p.312).
- Using a small, cheap, finetuned model to match or beat a much larger out-of-the-box model on a specific task (AIE p.312).
- [[model-distillation]]: finetuning a small model to imitate a larger model's behavior (AIE p.312).

## Reasons to hold off

- The benefits attributed to finetuning can often be achieved through careful [[prompt-engineering]] and [[context-construction]] instead (AIE p.312).
- Task-specific finetuning can hurt performance on other tasks (fixable via training on a full task mix, per-task models, or [[model-merging]]) (AIE p.312-313).
- It requires ongoing monitoring, maintenance, and a policy for deciding when a better base model justifies switching (AIE p.313).
- One historical motivation — reducing token usage by baking examples into weights instead of the prompt — has largely been superseded by [[prompt-caching]] (AIE p.315).

**Bottom line:** exhaust systematic prompt engineering first; if failures are informational, reach for RAG; only turn to finetuning when failures are behavioral (format, style, safety, narrow-domain adherence) and prompting/RAG haven't resolved them — and expect to combine RAG and finetuning in many real applications rather than choosing one exclusively (AIE p.311, p.317-318).

## Q18 What is the difference between data-centric AI and model-centric AI?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Model-centric AI improves performance by enhancing the model itself—designing new architectures, increasing [[model-size]], or developing new training techniques. Data-centric AI instead improves performance by enhancing the data—developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that let a better model be trained with fewer resources (AIE p.364).

This distinction is also reflected in how benchmarks have evolved: early deep learning benchmarks were largely model-centric, with a fixed dataset like ImageNet and researchers competing to train the best possible model on it. More recent benchmarks have shifted to being data-centric, fixing the model and having participants compete to build the dataset that yields the best performance—examples include Andrew Ng's 2021 data-centric AI competition, DataComp, DataPerf, and dcbench (AIE p.364).

However, the source treats this contrast as a framing device rather than a strict binary: meaningful progress in practice usually requires investment in both model and data improvements, not a choice of one over the other (AIE p.365).

## Q20 How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related but distinct quantities in measuring language model performance:

**Entropy** measures how much information, on average, a token carries, and how difficult it is to predict what comes next in a language. The lower a language's entropy, the more predictable it is, since each token carries less information (AIE p.119). Claude Shannon introduced entropy in 1951 as the average number of binary digits needed per letter of a language when translated into binary in the most efficient way (AIE p.119).

**Cross entropy** measures how difficult it is for a language model specifically to predict what comes next in a dataset, i.e., how far the model's learned distribution is from succeeding at learning the true distribution of that data (AIE p.120).

The precise mathematical relationship is: a model's cross entropy on training data depends on two things—the training data's own entropy H(P), and how far the model's learned distribution Q diverges from the true distribution P, measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence D_KL(P||Q). This gives the formula:

**H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q)** (AIE p.120)

Cross entropy is not symmetric: H(P, Q) differs from H(Q, P) (AIE p.120).

This relationship explains why a language model is trained to minimize its cross entropy with respect to the training data: if the model learns the training data's distribution perfectly, its cross entropy equals the training data's entropy, and the KL divergence term becomes 0. In this sense, cross entropy can be thought of as the model's approximation of the entropy of its training data — entropy is the theoretical floor, and cross entropy measures how close the model gets to that floor (AIE p.121).

More broadly, entropy underlies cross-entropy as well as perplexity, bits-per-character, and bits-per-byte — all four metrics are closely related, and knowing one lets you compute the other three given the necessary information (AIE p.119).
