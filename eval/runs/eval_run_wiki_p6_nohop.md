## Q1 What is quantization?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint (AIE p.328). For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is cheap, effective, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures; "low precision" generally refers to any format with fewer bits than standard FP32 (AIE p.328).

Strictly speaking, quantization means converting to an integer format, but the term is commonly used—including in this context—to refer to any precision-reduction technique (AIE p.328).

Two decisions define a quantization approach:

1. **What to quantize** — Weights and activations are the major memory contributors during inference. Weight quantization is more common than activation quantization since it has a more stable impact on performance with less accuracy loss (AIE p.328).

2. **When to quantize** — Quantization can happen post-training or during training. Post-training quantization (PTQ), quantizing a model after it is fully trained, is by far the most common approach and the one most relevant to application developers who do not train models themselves (AIE p.328).

**Benefits and tradeoffs:** Reduced precision speeds up computation by allowing larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, lowering both inference latency and training time, though format-conversion overhead can offset this gain (AIE p.329-330). The downside is accuracy loss — each precision conversion causes a small value change, many of which can compound into a large performance change, and out-of-range values can convert to infinity or an arbitrary value (AIE p.330).

Inference in lower precision is now standard practice: a model is trained in a higher-precision format to maximize performance, then quantized for inference, with PTQ offered for free by major frameworks such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Hugging Face transformers, as well as on-device frameworks like TensorFlow Lite and PyTorch Mobile (AIE p.330).

Quantization has a floor: values cannot use fewer than 1 bit, though 1-bit approaches have been attempted, such as BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, and BitNet (AIE p.329).

## Q3 What is model distillation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term. The goal has traditionally been to produce smaller models for deployment: deploying a big model can be resource-intensive, and distillation can yield a smaller, faster student that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395).

The student model can be trained from scratch, like DistilBERT, or finetuned from a pre-trained model, like Alpaca (AIE p.395). Synthetic instruction data is commonly used together with adapter-based techniques such as LoRA; for example, BuzzFeed finetuned a Flan-T5 model using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, reducing inference cost by 80%, though how well the model performed was unclear (AIE p.395).

A key defining boundary: distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard. Training a student on a teacher's synthetic outputs to become larger or more capable than the teacher (for example NVIDIA's Nemotron-4) is therefore not distillation—this separates distillation from synthetic-data training more generally (AIE p.396).

A practical limit on the technique is that many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models, so not all models can be distilled (AIE p.395).

## Q4 What's the difference between quantization and distillation?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Quantization and model distillation are both model-compression methods but work in fundamentally different ways:

**Quantization** reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's existing values (weights and/or activations), cutting memory footprint without changing the model's architecture or parameter count. A 10B-parameter model needs 40 GB in FP32 but only 20 GB in 16-bit format (AIE p.328). It is cheap, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures; it can be done post-training (the most common approach) or during training (AIE p.328). Reduced precision also speeds up computation and lowers latency, at the cost of some accuracy loss from value changes during conversion (AIE p.329-330).

**Model distillation** instead trains an entirely separate, smaller model (the student) to mimic the behavior of a larger model (the teacher), distilling the teacher's "knowledge" into the student (AIE p.395). The student can be trained from scratch (e.g., DistilBERT) or finetuned from a pre-trained model (e.g., Alpaca), and commonly uses synthetic data generated by the teacher (AIE p.395). The goal is a smaller, faster model whose performance is comparable to the teacher — critically, distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard, so training a student to exceed its teacher (e.g., NVIDIA's Nemotron-4) is not considered distillation (AIE p.396). It's also limited by licensing: many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models (AIE p.395).

**Key contrast:** Quantization keeps the same model but lowers the numerical precision of its values; distillation produces a different (smaller) model trained to imitate the original (AIE p.395, per model-distillation's Related section, which frames quantization as the "contrast: other main model-compression method; lowers numerical precision vs. trains a small student").

There's also a third related technique, **pruning**, which either removes nodes from the architecture or zeroes out less-useful parameters to increase sparsity — distinct from both quantization (which changes precision, not parameter values/structure) and distillation (which trains a new model) (AIE p.427).

## Q5 How do the chapters on evaluation and inference optimization relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki pages provided, the connection between evaluation and inference optimization is primarily structural rather than direct: both are components of the AI engineering stack, but they serve different roles.

**Shared placement in the stack**

Evaluation and inference optimization both sit within the responsibilities that make up the AI engineering process, though at different layers of the [[ai-stack]]:

- **Evaluation** is one of the three responsibilities of [[application-development]] (alongside prompt engineering and AI interface), and is described as needed "throughout the model-adaptation process: to select models, benchmark progress, decide whether an application is ready for deployment, and detect issues and opportunities in production" (AIE p.44).
- **Inference optimization** is one of the three main responsibilities of [[model-development]] (alongside modeling/training and dataset engineering) (AIE p.40, p.43). It means "making models faster and cheaper to run" (AIE p.43).

Notably, the page on [[model-development]] states that evaluation "is also required at this layer, but is more commonly encountered first at the application-development layer" (AIE p.40) — meaning evaluation is not exclusive to application development; it also matters when doing model-level work like inference optimization.

**A shared underlying tension: technique changes measured performance**

Both concepts converge on a common theme: the technique used can change the apparent quality of a model, complicating evaluation.

- On the evaluation side, "the existence of many adaptation techniques also complicates evaluation, since a system's apparent performance can shift dramatically with the technique used to evaluate it" — illustrated by the Gemini/ChatGPT MMLU prompting example (AIE p.44-45, see [[gemini-mmlu-prompting-comparison]]).
- On the inference-optimization side, the [[inference-optimization-levels]] page makes essentially the same point but for serving/optimization techniques: "Ideally, optimizing a model for speed and cost should not change its quality, but many optimization techniques can cause model degradation." It cites a Cerebras (2024) experiment where "the same Llama models achiev[ed] different benchmark performance when served by different inference-service providers, since providers may apply optimization techniques that alter model behavior" (AIE p.426).

So both chapters describe how the *choice of method* — whether a prompting technique in evaluation or an optimization technique in inference serving — can shift a model's measured performance, meaning evaluation results can't be interpreted independently of how the system was built or served.

**Why both matter more for foundation models**

Both topics are framed as long-standing ML engineering concerns that become *more* important with foundation models:
- Evaluation "has always mattered in ML engineering but matters even more with foundation models, chiefly because of their open-ended nature and expanded capabilities" (AIE p.44).
- Inference optimization "has always mattered in ML engineering, but foundation models' higher inference cost and latency make it even more important in AI engineering" (AIE p.43).

The wiki does not otherwise describe a direct dependency between the two topics (e.g., it does not state that inference-optimization techniques are evaluated using the same evaluation methods described in the evaluation page, beyond the parallel described above).

## Q7 What is RAG?

**Category:** factual

**A:** RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources — an internal database, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet (AIE p.253).

**Purpose and origin:** Because instructions are common to all queries while context is specific to each query, RAG constructs context per query instead of reusing the same context for every query. The book likens this to feature engineering for classical ML models, since both serve to give a model the information it needs to process an input (AIE p.255).

The retrieve-then-generate pattern was first introduced in "Reading Wikipedia to Answer Open-Domain Questions" (Chen et al., 2017), where a system retrieves the five Wikipedia pages most relevant to a question and a reader model uses them to generate an answer. The term "retrieval-augmented generation" itself was coined in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020), proposed for knowledge-intensive tasks where all available knowledge cannot be input into the model directly — only the information the retriever judges most relevant is retrieved and input into the model. Lewis et al. found that access to relevant information helps a model generate more detailed responses while reducing hallucinations (AIE p.254).

**Architecture:** A RAG system has two components: a retriever that retrieves information from external memory sources, and a generator that generates a response based on the retrieved information (AIE p.256). In the original RAG paper, the retriever and generative model were trained together, but in today's RAG systems the two components are often trained separately, with many teams building RAG systems from off-the-shelf retrievers and models (AIE p.256).

**Why it still matters despite long context:** RAG emerged early as a way to overcome models' context-length limitations. The book argues a sufficiently long context will not end RAG's usefulness, for two reasons: available data only grows over time, so context length will never expand fast enough for every application's data needs; and a model that can process long context does not necessarily use that context well, since longer contexts make a model more likely to focus on the wrong part, and every extra context token adds cost and potential latency. RAG lets a model use only the most relevant information per query, cutting input tokens while potentially increasing performance (AIE p.255).

## Q8 What are embeddings?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Embeddings are numerical representations, in vector form, that aim to capture the meaning of the original data (AIE p.134). Since computers work with numbers, models convert input into embeddings before processing it; many models, including GPTs and Llamas, include a step to generate embeddings, typically within a transformer architecture's embedding layer (AIE p.134). Embedding vector size is typically between 100 and 10,000 elements (AIE p.134).

An embedding algorithm is considered good if more-similar inputs have closer embeddings, measured by cosine similarity or related metrics -- for example, the embedding of "the cat sits on a mat" should be closer to "the dog plays on the grass" than to "AI research is super fun" (AIE p.135). Embedding quality can also be evaluated by its utility for a downstream task; embeddings are used in classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and RAG (AIE p.135).

Embeddings are not limited to text: ecommerce solutions have product embeddings, and Pinterest has embeddings for images, graphs, queries, and users (AIE p.135). A joint embedding space that represents data of different modalities is called a multimodal embedding space; in a text-image joint space, an image of a man fishing should embed closer to "a fisherman" than to "fashion show," enabling applications like text-based image search (AIE p.136).

Examples of models trained to produce embeddings include BERT (BERT base: 768, BERT large: 1024 embedding size), CLIP (image and text embeddings, both size 512), and Sentence Transformers (an open source model trained specifically to produce embeddings) (AIE p.134).

## Q9 What are the main techniques for inference optimization?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, inference optimization can be understood at multiple levels and through several specific techniques:

## Optimization Levels
[[inference-optimization-levels]] describes three levels at which inference optimization can occur, using an archery analogy: model-level (crafting better arrows), hardware-level (training a stronger archer), and service-level (refining the whole shooting process, including bow and aiming conditions) (AIE p.426). Hardware design is outside the book's scope, so only model-level and service-level techniques are discussed in detail, and production systems typically combine techniques from more than one level (AIE p.426).

## Model-Level Techniques
- **[[quantization]]** and **[[model-distillation]]** are cited as model-level compression techniques (AIE p.43, p.426).
- **[[kv-cache]]**: Since generating each token requires key/value vectors for all preceding tokens, the KV cache stores these vectors for reuse rather than recomputing them, though cache size grows with sequence length and batch size and can bottleneck long-context applications (AIE p.432-434). Techniques to improve attention efficiency fall into three buckets: redesigning the attention mechanism, optimizing KV cache management, and writing specialized attention kernels (AIE p.434).
- **Techniques addressing the [[autoregressive-decoding-bottleneck]]** (the fact that autoregressive models generate tokens sequentially, making generation slow and bandwidth-heavy) (AIE p.427-428):
  - **[[speculative-decoding]]**: A faster "draft" model generates K tokens, which a more powerful "target" model verifies in parallel, accepting the longest agreeing subsequence and generating one more token itself. This exploits idle FLOPs during memory-bound decoding and can more than halve latency without compromising quality (AIE p.428-429).
  - **[[inference-with-reference]]**: Mentioned as another technique addressing the decoding bottleneck (AIE p.428).
  - **[[parallel-decoding]]**: Mentioned as another technique that breaks the sequential dependency directly (AIE p.428).

## Service-Level Techniques
- **[[batching]]**: Groups multiple simultaneous requests for joint processing to improve throughput, at some cost to per-request latency (AIE p.440). Three main variants:
  - **Static batching** — waits until a fixed-size batch is full before processing (AIE p.440).
  - **Dynamic batching** — sets a maximum time window per batch, processing when full or the window elapses (AIE p.441).
  - **Continuous batching** (in-flight batching) — returns completed responses immediately and slots new requests into vacated spots, maximizing occupancy; introduced by the Orca paper (Yu et al., 2022) (AIE p.441).
- **[[prefill-and-decode]]** decoupling is noted as a related but distinct service-level concern addressing resource contention within request processing (see-also reference in batching page).

The overarching goal of all these techniques is to make models faster and cheaper to run, which matters more for foundation models due to their higher inference cost and latency (AIE p.43).

## Q11 What is backward propagation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Backpropagation (referred to as backward propagation) is the mechanism most neural networks are trained with (AIE p.320). Each training step has two phases:

1. **Forward pass** — computes the output from the input (AIE p.320).
2. **Backward pass** — updates the model's weights using signals aggregated from the forward pass (AIE p.320).

During inference, only the forward pass runs; during training, both the forward and backward passes run (AIE p.320).

The backward pass itself works in three steps (AIE p.321):

1. **Compute the loss** — the computed output is compared against the expected output (ground truth); the difference is the loss.
2. **Compute the gradient** — for each trainable parameter, the gradient (how much that parameter contributes to the loss) is computed as the derivative of the loss with respect to that parameter, giving one gradient value per trainable parameter. A parameter with a high gradient contributes significantly to the loss and should be adjusted more.
3. **Adjust parameter values** — parameters are updated according to their gradients, with the size of the adjustment determined by an [[optimizer]].

During the backward pass, each trainable parameter also carries additional stored values — its gradient and its optimizer states — so having more trainable parameters requires more memory to train (AIE p.321).

## Q13 What are the best practices of prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki pages provided, prompt engineering best practices break down into several categories:

## 1. Write Clear and Explicit Instructions

This is a core best practice with several concrete tips (AIE p.220):

- **Explain without ambiguity what the model should do** — e.g., specifying the exact scoring scale for grading an essay (1-5 vs 1-10), whether to guess or say "I don't know" when uncertain, and whether fractional outputs like 4.5 are allowed. Prompts are refined iteratively: observing an undesirable output motivates an explicit instruction ruling it out (AIE p.220).

- **Ask the model to adopt a persona**, which can shift the perspective it uses to generate a response. Example: an essay about liking chickens scores 2/5 with no persona but 4/5 when the model is told to respond as a first-grade teacher (AIE p.220).

- **Provide examples** to reduce ambiguity about the desired response (a form of [[few-shot-learning]]). Example: a children's chatbot debunks Santa without examples, but affirms Santa's existence once given a prior in-context example about the tooth fairy answered in-character (AIE p.221). Example formatting also matters for cost — a terser input-output arrow format ("chickpea --> edible") used 27 tokens versus 38 for a verbose format on the same classification task on GPT-4, and the cheaper format should be preferred when performance is equal (AIE p.222).

- **Specify the output format**, which matters for both cost/latency (longer outputs cost more per token and increase latency) and downstream parseability. This includes telling the model to skip preambles, specifying JSON keys, and using explicit end-of-prompt markers so the model knows where to begin its structured output rather than continuing to append to the input it was given (AIE p.222-223).

## 2. Give the Model Time to Think (Chain-of-Thought Prompting)

Explicitly asking a model to "think step by step" nudges it toward a more systematic approach to problem solving (AIE p.227). This can be done as:
- **Zero-shot CoT**: simply appending a phrase like "think step by step" or "explain your decision," letting the model work out its own steps (AIE p.227-228).
- **Specifying exact steps** to follow, or including a **worked example of the steps (one-shot CoT)** rather than leaving step generation zero-shot (AIE p.227-228).

CoT improved performance across models of different sizes (LaMDA, GPT-3, PaLM) and benchmarks (MAWPS, SVAMP, GSM-8K), and LinkedIn found it also reduces hallucinations (AIE p.227). A tradeoff is that CoT can increase perceived latency, since the model performs multiple intermediate steps before the first output token appears (AIE p.229).

## 3. Attend to Prompt Structure

A prompt generally consists of a task description, example(s), and the concrete task itself (AIE p.212). Where the task description should go depends on the model: most models, including GPT-4, perform better when it comes at the beginning of the prompt, while others, including Llama-3, perform better when it comes at the end (AIE p.213) — meaning best practices must be validated against the specific model in use.

## 4. Use In-Context Learning (Few-Shot/Zero-Shot)

Teaching a model what to do via prompts alone, without updating weights, by giving examples ("shots") in the prompt. How many examples are needed depends on the model and application, and is bounded by the model's maximum context length, since more examples lengthen the prompt and raise inference cost (AIE p.213).

## 5. Use System Prompts and User Prompts Deliberately

Splitting a prompt into a system prompt (task description, typically from application developers) and a user prompt (the task itself, typically from end users) is a common structural practice, often used for **roleplaying** — e.g., a real-estate chatbot given "You're an experienced real estate agent..." as its system prompt (AIE p.215). Developers can move instructions between the two creatively, though from the model's perspective they are ultimately concatenated into one final prompt (AIE p.215, p.217).

## Overarching Practice

Prompt engineering should be approached with the same rigor as any ML experiment — systematic experimentation and evaluation — and should be exhausted before moving to more resource-intensive techniques like finetuning. It is not sufficient on its own for production-ready applications and must be paired with statistics, engineering, and classic ML knowledge for experiment tracking, evaluation, and dataset curation (AIE p.211-212).

## Q14 What are the malicious attacks that can be done with prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, application developers must defend against three main types of prompt attacks (AIE p.235):

## 1. Prompt Extraction
A malicious user tries to extract an application's prompt, including its system prompt, either to replicate the application or to exploit it (AIE p.235).

## 2. Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection

**Jailbreaking** means subverting a model's safety features (e.g., getting a customer support bot to explain how to make a bomb). **Prompt injection** means injecting malicious instructions into a prompt so the model executes them instead of, or alongside, the legitimate request (e.g., turning "When will my order arrive?" into a database-deletion command). The book treats these together since they share the same goal and overlapping techniques (AIE p.238).

Techniques include:

- **Direct manual prompt hacking** (hand-crafted, social-engineering-style prompts):
  - *Obfuscation* — misspelling blocked keywords, mixing languages/Unicode, or appending password-like special-character strings to bypass filters (AIE p.239).
  - *Output formatting manipulation* — hiding malicious intent in unexpected formats, e.g. asking for a poem about hotwiring a car or a rap about robbing a house instead of asking directly (AIE p.239-240).
  - *Roleplaying* — asking the model to pretend to be a character/scenario exempt from its rules, e.g. the [[dan-jailbreak]] ("Do Anything Now"), the grandma exploit, an NSA-agent persona, or "Filter Improvement Mode" (AIE p.240).

- **Automated attacks** — algorithms that partially or fully automate prompt hacking, such as Zou et al.'s (2023) substring-substitution algorithm, or the [[pair-jailbreak-method]], an AI-powered approach often needing fewer than twenty queries (AIE p.240-241).

- **Indirect prompt injection** — placing malicious instructions in tools the model uses rather than in the user prompt directly:
  - *Passive phishing* — attackers leave malicious payloads in public spaces (web pages, GitHub repos, YouTube, Reddit) for the model to discover via tools like web search (AIE p.241-243).
  - *Active injection* — attackers proactively send malicious content to a target, e.g. an email instructing an email-reading assistant to forward every email in the inbox, or a poisoned database entry that gets interpreted as a delete command (AIE p.241-243).

## 3. Information Extraction
Exploiting a model's conversational interface to pull out training data or context information it wasn't meant to reveal, motivated by data theft, privacy violation, or copyright infringement (AIE p.243). Techniques include fill-in-the-blank prompting to trigger memorized data (AIE p.244), and divergence attacks like prompting a model to repeat a word forever until it diverges into verbatim training data (Nasr et al., 2023) (AIE p.244). This extends beyond text to images, e.g. extracting near-duplicate trademarked logos from Stable Diffusion (AIE p.245).

## Resulting Risks
These attacks can lead to remote code/tool execution, data leaks, social harms (e.g. weapon-making tutorials), misinformation, service interruption/subversion, and brand risk (AIE p.235-236).

## Q15 What is the future of comparative evaluation?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Comparative evaluation ranks models by pairwise comparison of their outputs rather than scoring each output independently, and though it faces challenges of scalability, standardization, and the fact that relative performance does not equal absolute performance, the technique has a **durable future** for three reasons (AIE p.155):

1. **It may become the only viable form of human judgment as models surpass human performance.** It is easier for human evaluators to judge which of two outputs is better than to assign each a concrete score. The [[llama-2]] paper reports that even when a model's writing exceeds the ability of the best human annotators, humans can still meaningfully compare two answers (Touvron et al., 2023) (AIE p.155).

2. **It targets the quality that actually matters — human preference.** This relieves the pressure to keep inventing new benchmarks to keep pace with expanding AI capability. Unlike benchmarks, which become useless once a model saturates them, comparative evaluation never saturates as long as newer, stronger models keep being introduced (AIE p.155) — see [[benchmark-saturation]].

3. **It is relatively hard to game.** There is no easy way to cheat it the way one can train a model on reference data to inflate a benchmark score. This is why many trust public comparative leaderboards (e.g., [[lmsys-chatbot-arena]]) more than other public leaderboards (AIE p.155).

Beyond these three durability arguments, comparative evaluation also surfaces discriminating signals unavailable elsewhere: for offline evaluation it complements evaluation benchmarks, and for online evaluation it complements A/B testing (AIE p.156).

## Q16 When should we finetune a model?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, finetuning is not a first resort but a decision made after other options have been tried and specific failure modes emerge. Here's the full picture:

## Try prompting first

Compared to prompt-based methods, finetuning requires significantly more resources — not just data and hardware, but also ML talent. Because of this cost, finetuning is generally attempted only after extensive experiments with prompt-based methods have been tried first (AIE p.311). Many complaints about prompting's ineffectiveness actually trace back to unclear instructions, unrepresentative examples, and poorly defined metrics — not a genuine limit of prompting (AIE p.314).

## The staged workflow

The book's recommended process moves from cheapest/simplest to more complex techniques, driven by evaluation at every step (AIE p.318):

1. Attempt the task with prompting alone (best practices, versioned prompts).
2. Add more examples to the prompt (typically 1–50, depending on use case).
3. If failures are information-based, connect the model to external data via RAG, starting with basic term-based retrieval like BM25.
4. Branch depending on the remaining failure mode: persistent information-based failures call for more advanced RAG (e.g., embedding-based retrieval); persistent **behavioral issues** (irrelevant, malformatted, or unsafe output) call for finetuning.
5. Combine RAG and finetuning together for further gains (AIE p.318-319).

## The core rule: finetuning is for form, RAG is for facts

- **Information-based failures** — outputs are factually wrong or outdated because the model lacks the information or its knowledge is stale — should be addressed with RAG (AIE p.316).
- **Behavior-based failures** — outputs are factually fine but irrelevant, malformatted, or stylistically wrong (e.g., HTML that doesn't compile, specs missing detail) — should be addressed with finetuning (AIE p.317).

Finetuning teaches a model to follow syntaxes and styles, and semantic-parsing tasks (which hinge on structured output formats) often require it because strong off-the-shelf models are weaker on less common syntaxes (AIE p.317). Research (Ovadia et al., 2024) found RAG actually outperforms finetuning on most MMLU categories across multiple models, and finetuning for one task can even degrade performance elsewhere — so RAG should generally be tried before finetuning when a model has both information and behavior issues, since RAG avoids the cost of curating training data or hosting finetuned models (AIE p.316-318).

## Specific reasons to finetune

- **Improving general or task-specific quality**, especially getting a model to follow specific output structures like JSON or YAML (AIE p.311).
- **Closing gaps a general-purpose model has** on a specific task/dialect/customer-specific queries it wasn't sufficiently trained on (AIE p.311).
- **Bias mitigation** — finetuning on carefully curated data can counteract biases inherited from training data (AIE p.312).
- **Using a small, cheap model to match or beat a larger one** on a narrow task, since smaller models are far cheaper/easier to finetune and run (AIE p.312), including via model distillation, where a small model is finetuned on a larger model's generated outputs (AIE p.312).

## Reasons to hold off

- Finetuning has high up-front and ongoing costs: annotated data, ML expertise to tune and debug training, and infrastructure/decisions for serving and inference optimization (AIE p.313).
- It requires an ongoing policy for monitoring and updating, since better base models are released faster than a finetuned model can typically be improved (AIE p.313).
- Finetuning on one task can degrade performance on others, requiring fixes like training on the full task mix, separate models per task, or model merging (AIE p.312-313).
- One historical benefit — avoiding repeated in-prompt examples to save tokens/latency/cost — has largely been replaced by prompt caching (AIE p.315).

## Bottom line

Both prompting and finetuning experiments benefit from the same systematic underpinnings: an evaluation pipeline, data-annotation guidelines, and experiment tracking (AIE p.314). Finetune once systematic prompting (and, if needed, RAG) experiments have proven inadequate — particularly when the remaining failures are about form/behavior rather than facts — and be prepared for the sustained cost of data, expertise, and maintenance that finetuning entails.

## Q18 What is the difference between data-centric AI and model-centric AI?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Model-centric AI improves performance by enhancing the model itself—designing new architectures, increasing model size, or developing new training techniques. Data-centric AI instead improves performance by enhancing the data—developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that let a better model be trained with fewer resources (AIE p.364).

Historically, this distinction played out in benchmarks: early deep learning benchmarks were largely model-centric, with a fixed dataset like ImageNet and researchers competing to train the best possible model on it. More recent benchmarks have shifted to being data-centric, fixing the model and having participants compete to build the dataset that yields the best performance—examples include Andrew Ng's 2021 data-centric AI competition, DataComp, DataPerf, and dcbench (AIE p.364).

That said, the book frames this less as a strict binary choice and more as a conceptual device: meaningful progress in practice usually requires investment in both model and data improvements, not choosing one over the other (AIE p.365).

## Q20 How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related quantities that both build on the idea of measuring the average information carried by tokens in a language.

**Entropy as the base concept**: Entropy H(P) measures how much information, on average, a token carries in a dataset with true distribution P — the lower the entropy, the more predictable the language is, since each token carries less information (AIE p.119-120).

**Cross-entropy builds on entropy**: A language model's cross entropy on training data depends on two things: (1) the training data's own entropy H(P), and (2) how far the model's learned distribution Q diverges from the true distribution P, measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence D_KL(P||Q). The relationship is:

H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q) (AIE p.120)

**What this means**: Cross entropy measures how difficult it is for a language model to predict what comes next in a dataset — essentially the model's attempt to approximate the entropy of its training data. Cross entropy is not symmetric, so H(P, Q) differs from H(Q, P) (AIE p.120).

**The connection in training**: A language model is trained to minimize its cross entropy with respect to the training data. If the model learns the training data's distribution perfectly (i.e., Q equals P), the KL divergence term becomes 0, and the model's cross entropy simply equals the training data's entropy H(P). Thus cross entropy can be thought of as the model's approximation of the entropy of its training data (AIE p.121). A language model encodes statistical information about token likelihood, and the better it learns this distribution, the lower its training cross-entropy becomes (AIE p.119).

In short: entropy is the underlying, irreducible unpredictability of the data itself, while cross-entropy is entropy plus an extra penalty (the KL divergence) for the model's imperfect approximation of that data's true distribution — and entropy underlies not just cross-entropy but also perplexity, bits-per-character, and bits-per-byte, all of which are interconvertible (AIE p.119).
