## Q1 What is quantization?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting its memory footprint. For example, a 10B-parameter model in 32-bit format requires 40 GB for its weights, but the same model in 16-bit format requires only 20 GB (AIE p.328). It is cheap, effective, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures; "low precision" generally refers to any format with fewer bits than standard FP32 (AIE p.328).

Strictly speaking, quantization means converting to an integer format, but the term is commonly used—and used in this way in the book—to refer to any precision-reduction technique more broadly (AIE p.328).

**What gets quantized and when**

Two decisions define a quantization approach: what to quantize, and when to quantize it.
- **What**: Weights and activations are the major memory contributors during inference. Weight quantization is more common than activation quantization since it has a more stable impact on performance with less accuracy loss (AIE p.328).
- **When**: Quantization can happen post-training or during training. Post-training quantization (PTQ) — quantizing a model after it is fully trained — is by far the most common approach and the one most relevant to application developers who do not train models themselves (AIE p.328).

**Benefits and costs**

Reduced precision also speeds up computation: it allows larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, which lowers both inference latency and training time, though format-conversion overhead can offset this gain (AIE p.329-330). The downside is accuracy loss — each precision conversion causes a small value change, many of which can compound into a large performance change, and out-of-range values can convert to infinity or an arbitrary value (AIE p.330).

Inference in lower precision is now standard practice: a model is trained in a higher-precision format to maximize performance, then quantized for inference, with PTQ offered for free by major frameworks such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Hugging Face transformers, as well as on-device frameworks like TensorFlow Lite and PyTorch Mobile (AIE p.330).

Training-time quantization is less common than PTQ but gaining traction, pursued for two distinct goals: producing a model that performs well in low precision at inference, or reducing training time and cost by training on cheaper hardware or training a larger model on the same hardware (AIE p.331). Quantization-aware training addresses the first goal; training directly in lower precision, often via mixed-precision training, can address both goals but is harder since backpropagation is more sensitive to lower precision (AIE p.331).

Quantization has a floor: values cannot use fewer than 1 bit, though 1-bit approaches have been attempted, such as BinaryConnect, Xnor-Net, and BitNet (AIE p.329).

## Q3 What is model distillation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher) (Hinton et al., 2015). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term (AIE p.395).

**Purpose:** The goal has traditionally been to produce smaller models for deployment — deploying a big model can be resource-intensive, and distillation can yield a smaller, faster student that retains performance comparable to the teacher (AIE p.395).

**How it's done:**
- The student model can be trained from scratch, as with DistilBERT, or finetuned from a pre-trained model, as with Alpaca (AIE p.395).
- Synthetic instruction data is commonly used together with adapter-based techniques such as LoRA; for example, BuzzFeed finetuned a Flan-T5 model using LoRA on examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, reducing inference cost by 80%, though how well the model performed was unclear (AIE p.395).

**Defining boundary:** Distillation implies the teacher's performance is the student's gold standard. Training a student on a teacher's synthetic outputs so that it becomes larger or more capable than the teacher (for example, NVIDIA's Nemotron-4) is therefore *not* distillation — this is the line that separates distillation from synthetic-data training in general (AIE p.396).

**Practical limit:** Not all models can be distilled, since many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other, especially competing, models (AIE p.395).

## Q4 What's the difference between quantization and distillation?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Quantization and model distillation are the two main model-compression methods discussed, and they work in fundamentally different ways:

**Quantization**
- Reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's existing values (weights and/or activations), cutting memory footprint without changing the model's architecture or parameter count. A 10B-parameter model needs 40 GB in FP32 but only 20 GB in 16-bit format (AIE p.328).
- It is cheap, straightforward, and generalizes across tasks and architectures (AIE p.328).
- Most commonly applied post-training (PTQ), i.e., after a model is fully trained, though it can also be done during training via quantization-aware training or mixed-precision training (AIE p.328, p.331).
- Also speeds up computation (larger batch sizes, faster arithmetic), lowering both latency and training time, though at the cost of some accuracy loss from precision conversion (AIE p.329-330).

**Model distillation**
- Trains an entirely new, smaller model (the student) to mimic the behavior of a larger model (the teacher), rather than compressing the existing model's numerical representation (AIE p.395).
- The student can be trained from scratch (e.g., DistilBERT) or finetuned from a pre-trained model (e.g., Alpaca) (AIE p.395).
- Relies on synthetic data generated by the teacher, and implies the teacher's performance is the student's "gold standard" -- if the student is trained to exceed the teacher (e.g., Nemotron-4), it isn't considered distillation (AIE p.395-396).
- Not always possible: many model licenses prohibit using a model's outputs to train other (especially competing) models (AIE p.395).

**Core contrast:** Quantization keeps the same model and architecture but represents its values with fewer bits; distillation produces a different, smaller model trained to imitate a larger one's outputs. As noted on the pruning page, quantization "reduces numerical precision rather than zeroing/removing parameters," and it is contrasted directly with distillation as the other main compression method — "lowers numerical precision vs. trains a small student" (model-distillation page, Related section, citing AIE p.395).

## Q7 What is RAG?

**Category:** factual

**A:** RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources—such as an internal database, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet (AIE p.253). Because instructions are common to all queries while context is specific to each query, RAG constructs context per query instead of reusing the same context for every query; the book likens this to feature engineering for classical ML models, since both serve to give a model the information it needs to process an input (AIE p.255).

**Origins**: The retrieve-then-generate pattern was first introduced in "Reading Wikipedia to Answer Open-Domain Questions" (Chen et al., 2017), where a system retrieves the five Wikipedia pages most relevant to a question and a reader model uses them to generate an answer. The term "retrieval-augmented generation" itself was coined in "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020), proposed for knowledge-intensive tasks where all available knowledge cannot be input into the model directly—only the information the retriever judges most relevant is retrieved and input into the model. Lewis et al. found that access to relevant information helps a model generate more detailed responses while reducing hallucinations (AIE p.254).

**Architecture**: A RAG system has two components: a retriever that retrieves information from external memory sources, and a generator that generates a response based on the retrieved information (AIE p.256).

**Why it still matters despite growing context lengths**: RAG emerged early as a way to overcome models' context-length limitations. The book argues a sufficiently long context will not end RAG's usefulness, for two reasons: available data only grows over time, so context length will never expand fast enough for every application's data needs; and a model that can process long context does not necessarily use that context well, since longer contexts make a model more likely to focus on the wrong part, and every extra context token adds cost and potential latency. RAG lets a model use only the most relevant information per query, cutting input tokens while potentially increasing performance (AIE p.255).

RAG is contrasted with the [[agent]] pattern—while RAG retrieves information from external data sources chiefly for context construction, agentic tool use (e.g., web search and news APIs) can do far more than construct context (AIE p.253).

## Q8 What are embeddings?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Embeddings are numerical representations, in vector form, that aim to capture the meaning of the original data (AIE p.134). Since computers work with numbers, models must convert input into embeddings before processing it; many models, including GPTs and Llamas, include a step to generate embeddings, typically within a transformer architecture's embedding layer (AIE p.134).

Key points about embeddings:

- **Vector size**: Embedding vector size is typically between 100 and 10,000 elements (AIE p.134).

- **Quality measures**: An embedding algorithm is considered good if more-similar inputs have closer embeddings, measured by cosine similarity or related metrics. For example, the embedding of "the cat sits on a mat" should be closer to "the dog plays on the grass" than to "AI research is super fun" (AIE p.135). Embedding quality can also be evaluated by its utility for downstream tasks; embeddings are used in classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and RAG (AIE p.135).

- **Not limited to text**: Ecommerce solutions have product embeddings, and Pinterest has embeddings for images, graphs, queries, and users (AIE p.135).

- **Multimodal embeddings**: A joint embedding space that represents data of different modalities is called a multimodal embedding space. In a text-image joint space, an image of a man fishing should embed closer to "a fisherman" than to "fashion show," enabling applications like text-based image search (AIE p.136).

Examples of dedicated embedding models include BERT (embedding sizes of 768 for base and 1024 for large), CLIP (which produces image and text embeddings, both size 512), and Sentence Transformers, an open source model trained specifically to produce embeddings (AIE p.134).

## Q9 What are the main techniques for inference optimization?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, inference optimization techniques are organized across several dimensions:

## Levels of Optimization

Inference optimization can be done at three levels: **model, hardware, and service** (AIE p.426). Using an archery analogy: model-level optimization is like crafting better arrows, hardware-level is like training a stronger archer, and service-level is like refining the entire shooting process (AIE p.426). Since hardware design is outside the book's scope, only model-level and service-level techniques are detailed, and in production, optimization typically combines techniques from more than one level (AIE p.426).

## Model-Level Techniques

- **[[quantization]]**: Reduces the number of bits used to represent a model's values, cutting memory footprint (e.g., a 10B-parameter model needs 40 GB in FP32 vs. 20 GB in 16-bit format) (AIE p.328). It also speeds up computation via larger batch sizes and faster arithmetic, though it risks accuracy loss (AIE p.328-330). It can be applied post-training (most common) or during training (AIE p.328, 331).

- **[[model-distillation]]**: Trains a smaller "student" model to mimic a larger "teacher" model, producing a smaller, faster model that retains comparable performance, reducing resource intensity of deployment (AIE p.395).

## Techniques Addressing the Autoregressive Decoding Bottleneck

Because autoregressive generation is slow (one token at a time) and memory-bandwidth-heavy, several techniques target this specific bottleneck (AIE p.427-428):

- **[[speculative-decoding]]**: A faster "draft" model generates candidate tokens that a more powerful "target" model verifies in parallel, exploiting idle FLOPs during memory-bandwidth-bound decoding (AIE p.428-429).
- **Inference with reference**
- **Parallel decoding**

## Service-Level Techniques

- **[[batching]]**: Groups multiple simultaneous inference requests for joint processing to improve throughput, at some latency cost. Three approaches exist: **static batching** (waits for a full batch), **dynamic batching** (fixed time window or full batch, whichever comes first), and **continuous batching**/in-flight batching (returns completed responses immediately and fills vacated slots with new requests) (AIE p.440-441).

## Underlying Framework

These techniques address two computational bottlenecks — **compute-bound** and **memory bandwidth-bound** workloads (AIE p.407-408). For transformers, prefill is compute-bound while decode is memory bandwidth-bound (AIE p.409), which is why decoding-specific techniques like speculative decoding are especially valuable — they effectively convert decoding's computation profile into that of prefilling, using otherwise idle compute (AIE p.429).

## Q11 What is backward propagation?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Backward propagation (backpropagation) is the mechanism most neural networks are trained with (AIE p.320).

**How it fits into training:** Each training step has two phases: a forward pass, which computes the output from the input, and a backward pass, which updates the model's weights using signals aggregated from the forward pass. During inference, only the forward pass runs; during training, both run (AIE p.320).

**The backward pass works in three steps** (AIE p.321):
1. The computed output is compared against the expected output (ground truth); the difference is the loss.
2. The gradient — how much each trainable parameter contributes to the loss — is computed as the derivative of the loss with respect to that parameter, giving one gradient value per trainable parameter. A parameter with a high gradient contributes significantly to the loss and should be adjusted more.
3. Parameter values are adjusted according to their gradients, with the adjustment size determined by an [[optimizer]].

**Memory implications:** During the backward pass, each trainable parameter carries additional stored values — its gradient and its optimizer states — so more trainable parameters require more memory to train (AIE p.321). For example, the [[adam-optimizer]], the dominant optimizer for transformer-based models, stores two additional values per trainable parameter (plus the gradient itself), for three stored values total per parameter (AIE p.323).

## Q13 What are the best practices of prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, prompt engineering best practices center on **writing clear and explicit instructions**, supported by related techniques for structure and reasoning. Here's the breakdown:

## Core principle
Prompt engineering is the process of crafting an instruction that gets a model to generate a desired outcome, without changing model weights (unlike finetuning). It should be exhausted before moving to more resource-intensive techniques like finetuning, and despite seeming simple, it requires the same experimental rigor as any ML experiment (AIE p.211-212).

## 1. Write clear and explicit instructions
This breaks into four concrete tips (AIE p.220-223):

- **Remove ambiguity**: Specify exactly what the model should do — e.g., the exact scoring scale (1-5 vs 1-10), whether fractional outputs are allowed, and whether the model should guess or say "I don't know" when uncertain. Prompts are refined iteratively based on observed undesirable outputs (AIE p.220).
- **Ask the model to adopt a persona**: This shifts the perspective used to generate a response — e.g., an essay scored 2/5 with no persona jumped to 4/5 when the model was told to respond as a first-grade teacher (AIE p.220).
- **Provide examples**: This reduces ambiguity about the desired response and is a form of few-shot learning — e.g., giving a children's chatbot a prior in-context example about the tooth fairy flipped its answer about Santa from debunking to affirming. Example formatting also affects cost: a terser format used 27 tokens vs. 38 for an equivalent classification prompt on GPT-4, so the cheaper format should be preferred when performance is equal (AIE p.221-222).
- **Specify the output format**: This matters for cost/latency (longer outputs cost more and increase latency) and downstream parseability — e.g., telling the model to skip preambles, specifying JSON keys, and using explicit end-of-prompt markers so the model knows where to begin its structured output (AIE p.222-223).

## 2. Attend to prompt structure
A prompt generally has a task description, examples, and the concrete task itself. Model developers have found structural placement matters and varies by model: most models, including GPT-4, perform better with the task description at the beginning, while others, like Llama 3, perform better with it at the end (AIE p.212-213).

## 3. Use in-context learning (zero-shot and few-shot)
Providing examples ("shots") in the prompt teaches desired behavior without weight updates. More examples generally help, bounded by the model's maximum context length and rising inference cost. Few-shot examples matter most for domain-specific use cases, such as APIs a model has seen little of during training (AIE p.213-214).

## 4. Give the model time to think (chain-of-thought prompting)
Explicitly asking the model to "think step by step" nudges it toward a more systematic approach and has been shown to improve performance across model sizes and benchmarks, and to reduce hallucinations. This can be zero-shot (just adding the phrase) or one-shot (providing a worked example of the reasoning steps) (AIE p.227-228). This technique, like prompt decomposition, can increase perceived latency since the model performs intermediate steps before the first output token appears (AIE p.229).

## Underlying requirement
All of these techniques assume the model has sufficient **instruction-following capability** — a model that can't follow instructions well won't respond correctly no matter how the prompt is structured (AIE p.213, p.172).

## Q14 What are the malicious attacks that can be done with prompt engineering?

**Category:** factual

**A:** Based on the wiki, application developers must defend against three main types of prompt attacks (AIE p.235):

## 1. Prompt Extraction
A malicious user tries to extract an application's prompt, including its system prompt, either to replicate the application or to exploit it (AIE p.235).

## 2. Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection
These are treated together since they share the same goal — getting a model to express undesirable behavior — with overlapping techniques (AIE p.238):

- **Jailbreaking**: Subverting a model's safety features, e.g. getting a customer support bot to explain how to make a bomb (AIE p.238).
- **Prompt injection**: Injecting malicious instructions into a prompt so the model executes them instead of, or alongside, the legitimate request, e.g. turning "When will my order arrive?" into a database-deletion command (AIE p.238).

Specific techniques include:
- **Direct manual prompt hacking** (hand-crafted, social-engineering-style attacks): 
  - *Obfuscation* — misspelling blocked keywords, mixing languages/Unicode, or appending password-like special-character strings to bypass filters (AIE p.239).
  - *Output formatting manipulation* — hiding malicious intent in unexpected formats, e.g. asking for a poem about hotwiring a car or code for a Molotov cocktail (AIE p.239-240).
  - *[[roleplaying]]* — asking the model to pretend to be a character exempt from its rules, e.g. the [[dan-jailbreak]] ("Do Anything Now") or the grandma exploit (AIE p.240).
- **Automated attacks** — algorithms that partially or fully automate prompt hacking, such as random substring substitution (Zou et al., 2023) or the [[pair-jailbreak-method]], an AI-powered approach often needing fewer than twenty queries (AIE p.240-241).
- **Indirect prompt injection** — placing malicious instructions in tools the model uses rather than directly in the user prompt:
  - *Passive phishing* — leaving malicious payloads in public spaces (web pages, GitHub repos, etc.) for the model to discover via tools like web search (AIE p.241-243).
  - *Active injection* — proactively sending a target malicious content, e.g. an email instructing an email-reading assistant to forward every email in the inbox, or a poisoned database entry that gets interpreted as a delete command (AIE p.241-243).

## 3. Information Extraction
Exploiting a model's conversational interface to pull out training data or context information it wasn't meant to reveal, motivated by data theft, privacy violation, or copyright infringement (AIE p.243). Techniques include fill-in-the-blank prompting to trigger memorization (e.g. "X's email address is _") and divergence attacks, such as prompting ChatGPT to repeat a word forever until it diverges into verbatim training data (Nasr et al., 2023) (AIE p.244).

## Risks of These Attacks
These attacks can lead to (AIE p.235-236):
- Remote code or tool execution
- Data leaks
- Social harms (e.g. weapon-making tutorials)
- Misinformation
- Service interruption and subversion
- Brand risk (offensive outputs causing PR crises)

## Q15 What is the future of comparative evaluation?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, comparative evaluation has a durable future for three main reasons (AIE p.155-156):

**1. It matches human evaluative capability better than scoring**
It is easier for human evaluators to judge which of two outputs is better than to assign each output a concrete score. As models surpass human performance, this may become the only viable form of human judgment: the [[llama-2]] paper reports that even when a model's writing exceeds the ability of the best human annotators, humans can still meaningfully compare two answers (Touvron et al., 2023) (AIE p.155).

**2. It never saturates**
Comparative evaluation targets the quality that actually matters -- human preference -- which relieves the pressure to keep inventing new benchmarks to keep pace with expanding AI capability. Unlike benchmarks, which become useless once a model saturates them (see [[benchmark-saturation]]), comparative evaluation never saturates as long as newer, stronger models keep being introduced (AIE p.155).

**3. It is hard to game**
There is no easy way to cheat comparative evaluation the way one can train a model on reference data to inflate a benchmark score. This is why many trust public comparative leaderboards, such as [[lmsys-chatbot-arena]], more than other public leaderboards (AIE p.155).

**Additional value**
Comparative evaluation can also surface discriminating signals unavailable elsewhere: for offline evaluation it complements evaluation benchmarks, and for online evaluation it complements A/B testing (AIE p.156).

**Caveats it must still contend with**
Despite this durable future, comparative evaluation faces ongoing challenges of scalability, standardization, and the fact that relative performance does not equal absolute performance (AIE p.155) -- these remain open limitations rather than resolved issues.

**Related development**
One response to the expense of collecting human preference signals is the [[preference-model]], a specialized AI judge trained to predict which of two responses a human would prefer, supplying the same kind of signal that comparative evaluation and RLHF alignment both require, automatically and more cheaply than human labeling (AIE p.156).

## Q16 When should we finetune a model?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Based on the wiki, "when to finetune" is not a simple yes/no decision but a staged, evidence-driven judgment. Here is the full picture:

## Finetuning is generally a later resort, not a first step

Finetuning requires significantly more resources than prompt-based methods — not just data and hardware, but ML talent — so it is generally attempted only after extensive experiments with prompt-based methods have been tried first (AIE p.311). It is rarely the right first step in a new project, since it requires high up-front investment (annotated data, expertise to tune training knobs and debug training, and serving infrastructure) plus ongoing maintenance as better base models are released (AIE p.313).

The recommended path is to start with prompting, and only explore finetuning once systematic, well-designed prompt experiments prove inadequate — many complaints about prompting's ineffectiveness actually trace back to unclear instructions, unrepresentative examples, or poorly defined metrics rather than a genuine limit of prompting (AIE p.314).

## The staged workflow before finetuning

The book's suggested model-adaptation workflow is: (1) prompting alone, (2) add more examples to the prompt (roughly 1–50), (3) if failures are information-based, add RAG starting with basic term-based retrieval, (4) branch based on the remaining failure mode, (5) potentially combine RAG and finetuning (AIE p.318-319).

## The key decision rule: form vs. facts

The book's summary rule is **"finetuning is for form, and RAG is for facts"** (AIE p.317). Concretely:

- **Finetune when failures are behavior-based** — outputs are factually fine but irrelevant, malformatted, or stylistically wrong (e.g., HTML that doesn't compile, technical specs missing needed detail), or when the task requires a specific output structure like JSON/YAML or an uncommon syntax (semantic parsing) (AIE p.311, p.317).
- **Use RAG instead when failures are information-based** — outputs are factually wrong or outdated because the model lacks the information or its knowledge is stale (AIE p.316-317). Ovadia et al. (2024) found RAG outperforms finetuning even on tasks that look like knowledge tasks, across MMLU categories on three different models (AIE p.316-317).

## Specific legitimate reasons to finetune

- To improve general or task-specific quality where a general-purpose model performs well on broad benchmarks but fails on a narrower task it wasn't sufficiently trained on (e.g., an uncommon SQL dialect or customer-specific queries) (AIE p.311).
- To mitigate bias, by finetuning on carefully curated data to counteract biases inherited from training data (AIE p.312).
- To produce a small, cheap, fast model that outperforms a much larger out-of-the-box model on a specific task, since smaller models are far more common and practical to finetune (AIE p.312).
- To distill a larger model's behavior into a smaller student model using data the larger model generated (model distillation) (AIE p.312).

## Reasons to hold off

Many gains attributed to finetuning can be achieved through careful prompt engineering and context construction instead (AIE p.312). Finetuning for one task can degrade performance on others, it demands ongoing investment and maintenance, and one of its historical benefits — shortening prompts by baking in examples — has been largely superseded by prompt caching (AIE p.312-315).

## Bottom line

Finetune once prompting (and, if information is the issue, RAG) has been tried thoroughly and the remaining failures are behavioral/stylistic (structure, format, domain-specific competence, safety, or bias) rather than factual — and be prepared for the added cost of data curation, training expertise, and ongoing model serving/maintenance that finetuning entails (AIE p.311-319).

## Q18 What is the difference between data-centric AI and model-centric AI?

**Category:** comparison

**A:** Model-centric AI improves performance by enhancing the model itself — designing new architectures, increasing [[model-size]], or developing new training techniques. Data-centric AI instead improves performance by enhancing the data — developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that let a better model be trained with fewer resources (AIE p.364).

This distinction is reflected in how benchmarks have evolved: early deep learning benchmarks were largely model-centric, with a fixed dataset (like ImageNet) and competition over who could train the best model on it. More recent benchmarks are data-centric, fixing the model and having participants compete to build the best dataset — for example, Andrew Ng's 2021 competition (improving a fixed base dataset by fixing labels, adding edge cases, augmenting data) and DataComp (having participants build the best training dataset for a CLIP model, scored across 38 downstream tasks, later extended to language models from 412M to 7B parameters) (AIE p.364).

However, the book frames this less as a strict binary and more as a framing device: meaningful progress in practice usually requires investment in both model and data improvements, not a choice of one approach over the other (AIE p.365).

## Q20 How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**Category:** synthesis

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related quantities in measuring language predictability:

**Entropy as the base concept:** Entropy measures how much information, on average, a token carries — the higher a language's entropy, the more information each token carries, and the more bits are needed to represent it. Lower entropy means a language is more predictable, since a token then carries less information (AIE p.119-120).

**Cross-entropy builds on entropy:** A language model encodes statistical information about how likely a token is to appear in a given context; the better it learns this distribution, the better it predicts what comes next in the training data, and the lower its training cross-entropy (AIE p.119).

**The mathematical relationship:** A model's cross entropy on training data depends on two things: the training data's own entropy H(P), and how far the model's learned distribution Q diverges from the true distribution P, measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence D_KL(P||Q). Specifically, the model's cross entropy is:

H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P||Q) (AIE p.120)

**What this means in practice:** A language model is trained to minimize its cross entropy with respect to the training data. If it learns the training data's distribution perfectly, its cross entropy equals the training data's entropy, and the KL divergence term becomes 0. So cross entropy can be thought of as the model's approximation of the entropy of its training data (AIE p.121).

**Asymmetry:** Unlike entropy, cross entropy is not symmetric — H(P, Q) differs from H(Q, P) (AIE p.120).

In short, entropy measures the inherent predictability/information content of the true data distribution, while cross-entropy measures how well a model's learned distribution matches that true distribution — with entropy being the theoretical floor a model's cross-entropy approaches as it learns the data perfectly (AIE p.119-121).
